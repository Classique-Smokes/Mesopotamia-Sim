**Principal answer.** In the examined Nippur dossiers, continuity beyond an individual is visible chiefly in relationships invoked by later documents, inherited or reassigned rights, obligations, and persistent claims. Adoption was one means of establishing succession, but it operated alongside earlier family connections, gifts, partition, exchange, compensation and subsequent conveyance. Some connections were deliberately terminated. These histories do **not**, by themselves, demonstrate an unchanged group living in the same house. This is an interpretation of the cases below, not a citywide definition of household. [C-C01–C-C22; D01–D18, D25]

## 2. Corpus and method actually used

### 2.1 What was examined

The retained document inventory contains **27 records**. Text was consulted for **17 distinct artifacts**, representing **15 legal acts after deduplicating parallel witnesses**. Two of those acts, TIM 4, 13 and 14, have explicitly uncertain Nippur provenance and are supplementary. The two principal cases and judicial countercase therefore rest on **15 textual artifacts representing 13 acts**. Ten other records are catalogue-only, indirectly reported, or an envelope association checked through a published collation; they are not counted as directly read ancient acts. [Document ledger; S17; D01–D27]

The first cluster is the Mannum-mešu-liṣṣur dossier and related earlier title/family documents, principally OECT 8 and MC 3, with a later BE 6/2 conveyance. The second is the Narāmtum–Ur-Pabilsaga–Narubtum dossier, anchored by TMH 10, 6 and the parallel estate-division witnesses TMH 10, 18a/b. The supporting judicial case is SAOC 44, 24. These are **documentary clusters**, not three independently verified, completely excavated residential archives. [D01–D18, D25; S05–S08]

The main interpretive works actually consulted were Meinhold’s 2014 and 2015 studies, Van Wyk’s 2019 case study, and Földi’s 2019 review with new collations. Selected digital primary editions were checked against these discussions. Stone and Owen’s complete 1991 book and Goddeeris’s complete 2016 volumes were **not** read in full. Their selected texts were reached through digital editions or subsequent publications. Meinhold’s 2020 chapter was available only through bibliographic/indexed material, but the underlying revised court text was read directly in CDLI. The source ledger differentiates these access levels. [S01–S10; D18]

### 2.2 Chronology and provenance

The earliest core text is dated to **Lipit-Enlil d**; the latest core conveyance is assigned to **Samsu-iluna 29** in the edition-based reconstruction, although its CDLI year field remains uncertain. Most of the central sequence falls in the reigns of Hammurabi and Samsu-iluna, with the second case beginning in Rīm-Sîn I 43. This is principally nineteenth- and eighteenth-century evidence within the approved research frame, not coverage extending evenly to 1600 BCE. Regnal dates are retained rather than presenting uncertain absolute conversions as exact dates. Rīm-Sîn **I** and Rīm-Sîn **II** are distinguished. [D01, D04–D15, D25; S01]

Most catalogues give Nippur attribution without an independently recoverable house, locus or level. MC 3, 51 has uncertain catalogue provenience and is associated with Nippur through its dossier. The court tablet has excavation number **3N-T0873**, but the exact residential context was not established from the consulted material. TIM 4, 13–14 have a broad Nippur header but a detailed provenience field marked uncertain. None of these absences is silently filled from a presumed family residence. [D06, D18, D26–D27]

### 2.3 Evidentiary method

Each historical assertion was separated into three possible layers: **what an instrument records; what a specialist reconstructs from several instruments; and the interpretation offered in this return**. A legal instrument is direct evidence that a relationship, claim or transaction was formally represented in that way. It is not an unmediated record of all the people’s daily conduct. A prospective clause, retrospective recital and later settlement therefore have different evidentiary force even when they concern the same type of support or property. [Method applied to D01, D08, D12, D14, D18]

The direct core consists of legal records rather than a set of school-model contracts. No didactic example has been counted as an event. Van Wyk’s use of scribal templates is examined as an interpretation of how an actual agreement was composed, not as permission to turn a model formula into an observed transaction. Neither the number of legal acts nor the number of individual tablets is treated as a count of households. [S03, S10; document ledger]

Names were compared with filiation, co-participants, witnesses, dates and the property history. A repeated name alone does not establish identity. In particular, the two women called Narubtum in the two principal dossiers remain separate. The Munawwirum who is an heir of Ibbī-Enlil is not automatically the Munawwirum reconstructed as Mannum’s uncle. Published restorations and inconsistent seal readings are retained as problems rather than silently normalized. [D06–D08, D11, D14, D25; S01]

Most inspected texts use Sumerian legal formulations; the expense statement in OECT 8, 11 includes Akkadian. The TMH catalogue’s Akkadian labels do not accurately describe the visible Sumerian formulation of every item. English descriptions here are analytical paraphrases of published digital texts, not new critical editions or a claim to specialist collation of the clay. No OCR was used. PDF tables and diagrams used in the source critique were checked visually; the inaccessible excavation plans were not. [D08, D12, D14; S03–S04]

### 2.4 Selection bias and the stopping boundary

This is a **purposive, source-critical case corpus**, selected for linked persons, recoverable dates and relevant transactions. It overrepresents people with property, institutional interests and reasons to preserve legal evidence. It cannot supply adoption rates, average ages, normal household size, mortality, incidence of conflict or the relative importance of adoption across Nippur. Enslaved persons appear chiefly through others’ transactions; their appearance is not a representative account of their own family lives. [Document inventory; D12, D14–D15]

The stopping point is a completed reviewable return, not an exhaustive census of Nippur texts. Archive extensions and inaccessible publications are retained as explicit questions. In particular, a secondary reconstruction is not upgraded to direct evidence merely because the primary volume could not be retrieved.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 What can “continuity” mean in these sources?

For this investigation, four questions must be answered separately: **Which relationships are invoked later? Which resources or claims pass onward? Which obligations are stipulated or subsequently recognized? Which people can actually be shown to occupy a place together?** This is an analytical separation, not a proposed classification system for the project. A positive answer to the first three does not logically establish the fourth.

The distinction is particularly important for institutional rights conventionally called **prebends**. The inspected texts often express such rights as parts of an annual cycle and associate them with named gates, offices or cultic settings. Their transfer is documented. How often a holder personally performed service, who supplied substitute labour, and what income actually resulted are different questions. The term must not be read as a modern salary, job contract or unrestricted cash-producing asset. [D01, D07, D25]

The cases below also distinguish **a relationship’s creation** from **its later invocation**. An adoption clause embedded in a division can explain why somebody has standing in that division; it need not record a new adoption at that later moment. Similarly, describing someone as an heir does not, without more, tell us the date of a predecessor’s death or whether the person already controlled the entire estate. [D04, D06, D12, D14]

### 4.2 Microhistory A — Mannum-mešu-liṣṣur and the changing composition of family resources

#### A. The earliest linked succession episode: Ur-Lumma and Lu-Bau

OECT 8, 20 records **Ur-Lumma**, son of Lu-Inana, adopting **Lu-Bau**, identified as son of E-lūti, as heir. It combines property and institutional rights with stipulated provisions during Ur-Lumma’s lifetime. The provisions include monthly grain and oil and annual wool; the stated quantities are **4 ban of barley and 4 sila of oil per month, and 4 mina of wool per year**. The text is evidence for a deliberately linked support-and-succession arrangement, not proof of the adopter’s age, childlessness, or successful receipt of every instalment. [D01, obv. 1–14; rev. 1–9]

Later parallel divisions, OECT 8, 17 and 18, concern **Nanna-meša and Adda-kalla**. The seals on no. 18 identify both as sons of Lu-Bau; portions of the estate explicitly refer back to Ur-Lumma. This supplies a stronger link than merely finding recurring occupational titles. The documents allocate built property, open plots, agricultural land, institutional shares and movable objects. Doors and wagon components are among the objects treated separately. They do not establish that an entire residence and all its occupants passed intact to one successor. [D02–D03, especially D03 obv. 1–24; rev. 1–22; seals]

The significance is a **documented title and relationship history across successive allocations**. It would be too strong to infer that the same residential group persisted from Ur-Lumma to all later parties. The record also does not justify making *ibila*, “heir,” the exclusive marker of adoption while treating *dumu*, “son,” as an unambiguous biological category. Different legal and identifying contexts can emphasize different aspects of a person’s position. [D01–D03; S01, discussion of the earlier divisions]

#### B. A daughter’s earlier gift remains consequential

A Hammurabi 31 settlement recalls a lifetime gift by **Nanna-meša to his daughter Nin-kuzu**. It concerns **15 gin of built property**, compensation, and the renunciation of future claims by **Awīlīja and Munawwirum**, identified as heirs of Nuska-amaḫ. Nuska-amaḫ himself is identified as Nanna-meša’s son. This is not simply a list of equal shares issued at a father’s death: a previous act had to be acknowledged in a later configuration of claimants. [D04, obv. 1–20; rev. 1; seal]

The transaction shows why “inheritance” is too narrow when used to exclude gifts during life, compensatory payments or waivers. A later heir could encounter already-created rights, rather than a clean estate available for division from the beginning. Conversely, nothing here establishes a universal entitlement of daughters to the same allocation, a standard dowry rule, or the residence of Nin-kuzu in the property concerned. Those would require further evidence. [D04; C-C09]

#### C. Mannum’s heir appointment is not the whole explanation

In Samsu-iluna 6, Nannāja’s agreement with **Lamassum**, daughter of Ipqu-Ea, combines marriage with the appointment of **Mannum-mešu-liṣṣur, Ninurta-muballiṭ, Sîn-māgir and Ina-Ekur-rabi** as heirs. The damaged silver provision and the people’s wider family relationships have generated divergent reconstructions. The text does not justify asserting that all four were Lamassum’s biological sons or that Mannum obtained all subsequent resources solely through this agreement. [D05; S01, discussion of MC 3, 27]

A separate adoption in Samsu-iluna 11 identifies **Ninurta-muballiṭ, son of Awīlīja**, with the couple **Ilī-ippalsam and Elmēšum**. It includes a silver contribution and reciprocal repudiation terms. Identification with the similarly named year-6 heir is plausible in the dossier, but the surviving sequence does not tell us whether earlier affiliations had ended, remained operative, or were legally reconfigured. A later document is not automatically evidence of several simultaneously valid adoptions. [D09; D05; S01]

#### D. The key chronological correction

**MC 3, 51 must be read in light of Meinhold’s re-edition, which dates it to Samsu-iluna 10 rather than the older year 23.** It records division of an estate deriving from **Nuska-amaḫ**, with Mannum and Munawwirum as the relevant successors. The year-10 exchange discussed below explicitly refers to Mannum’s inherited and preferential property. Together, these sources put inheritance into the documented sequence before the later concentration of purchases, rather than after it. [D06; D07; S01, pp. 26–27]

The causal conclusion must remain bounded. The revised order undermines an argument that inheritance was too late to contribute to Mannum’s accumulation. It does **not** identify the cash used for every purchase or prove that inheritance income financed them. Adoption, inherited property, past expenses, exchanges and purchases are distinct potential contributors. Meinhold’s reconstruction gives family inheritance a larger role than an adoption-only account; this return accepts the chronological correction without importing an unverified income model. [S01; D05–D10]

The earlier Hammurabi 31 text already has heirs acting for Nuska-amaḫ’s succession, whereas the partition is substantially later. The resulting reconstruction is compatible with delayed estate division and a successor acting in place of a deceased predecessor. It is not a direct observation of uninterrupted joint management in every intervening year. The missing documents could conceal earlier allocations or arrangements. Date of partition, date of death, and duration of actual joint administration must remain separate. [D04, D06; S01]

#### E. Inherited property changes form

On Samsu-iluna 10/03/23, Mannum exchanged **12 gin of built property**, explicitly associated with his inherited and preferential share, for annual institutional fractions held by Munawwirum: **10½ days at the great gate, 5 days at Ningagia’s gate, and 1½ days in the Ninlil-related entitlement**. A later correction affects the precise designation of the latter. The secure point is the exchange between different resource types, not a measured increase in annual income. [D07, obv. 1–12; S01, appendix commentary]

Meinhold identifies the other party as Mannum’s uncle and co-heir. The displayed digital seal restoration does not straightforwardly agree with that filiation. The relation is therefore retained as a published reconstruction, not an independently repaired reading. The exchange itself remains much more secure than a fully resolved genealogy. It shows that continuity of resources could be achieved through **substitution**, not only preservation of the same asset. [D06–D07; S01]

#### F. An expense claim reaches a later group of heirs

OECT 8, 19 allocates institutional shares to **Ninurta-muštāl, Namāršu-lūmur and Munawwirum**. Its date is Rīm-Sîn II 3/10/25, placed in the Samsu-iluna 9 sequence by Meinhold. The digital father-name reading is **Ibni-Enlil**, whereas the linked subsequent expense settlement has **Ibbī-Enlil**; the recurring group of heirs supports the association, but the variant is not suppressed. [D25, rev. 1–14 and seal; S01]

In the following settlement, Mannum states expenses involving **2 gur of barley and food for one year** for their father. His expense tablet is examined, and the heirs convey **one month per year of the Enlil/Ninlil é-da-di entitlement**, with a waiver. This is stronger evidence than a promise of future care: a later agreement recognizes a claim grounded in reported past provision. It still does not establish the beneficiary’s age, the exact reason for the expenditure, or an adoption by him. [D08, obv. 1–16; rev. 1–6]

The distinction matters causally. This transfer need not be explained as the automatic fulfilment of a filial inheritance right. It may reflect settlement of an expense claim against successor-side parties. The wider lesson is that a person’s provision for another could become part of an estate’s later legal history without proving either common residence or adoptive kinship. The Munawwirum among these heirs must not simply be merged with the uncle bearing the same name. [D08, D25; S01]

#### G. A property history continues after Mannum disappears from the transaction

BE 6/2, 64 records **Ninurta-rāʾim-zērim** recovering paternal property from **Ninurta-muballiṭ, Iddin-Ištar and Narubtum**, while recalling an earlier purchase by Mannum from the Ea-iddinam side. Iddin-Ištar is identified as Mannum’s son and Narubtum as his mother. The seller-side succession and Mannum’s death are reconstructed from the configuration, not narrated as a dated death event. The year-29 attribution is retained with its editorial qualification. [D11]

The proposed association with Mannum’s earlier purchase, BE 6/2, 38, is reasonable but was not fully checked against that tablet’s text. Nor does the presence of a mother among vendors decide whether she was acting as guardian, co-owner, a necessary consenting party, or in another capacity. What is secure is a conveyance reconnecting property to an earlier owner’s family. Its physical occupancy and the precise history of the built versus open plot remain unresolved. [D10, catalogue only; D11; S02]

**Case A assessment.** This is a linked history of succession claims and changes in resource composition, not a demonstrated biography of one unchanging house. Its most important corrections are the revised inheritance chronology, the distinction between support expenditure and adoption, and the refusal to turn a chain of title into continuous co-residence. The dossier contains both persistence and redirection: prior relationships matter, but assets can be partitioned, substituted or conveyed to another family. [D01–D11, D25; C-C06–C-C14]

### 4.3 Microhistory B — Narāmtum, Ur-Pabilsaga and Narubtum

#### A. A female adopter with an earlier property history

TMH 10, 6, dated Rīm-Sîn 43/month 8, identifies **Narāmtum**, daughter of Lu-Nanna and **nadītu of Lugal-aba**, as the adopter of **Ur-Pabilsaga, son of Ubārum**. Here *nadītu* is an explicitly attested religious status; assumptions about age, celibacy, fertility or the practice of all such women are not required for the argument. [D12, obv. 1–5]

The property list includes **5/6 sar of built property**, **½ iku and 20 sar of garden**, **1½ iku and 10 sar of field**, and named dependent persons. The text attributes the property to a gift by Narāmtum’s father. Calling it simply an inheritance would erase that distinction. Some personal-status and relationship labels in the list are damaged and have been collated; the report does not invent a complete family tree for the listed dependents. [D12, obv. 6–17; S04, p. 251]

Ur-Pabilsaga is to supply grain, oil and wool during Narāmtum’s life. The quantities are not specified in the inspected wording. The agreement anticipates repudiation from either side. It thus associates material provision, affiliation and property, but it does not establish that every transfer awaited death, that all obligations were performed, or that the two lived in one building. [D12, obv. 18–rev. 11]

#### B. What is, and is not, known between the endpoints

Specialists connect the dossier to an earlier division **ARN 41**, dated Rīm-Sîn 53/month 8, and additional gifts and transactions. Its correct consulted catalogue identifier is **Ni 1923**. The edition text was not independently read, so this report does not present it as a fully verified intervening episode. TMH 10, 24 and further marriage, sale and dispute documents remain leads rather than filled-in chapters of a seamless biography. [D13, D17, D20–D24; S03–S04]

This restriction is consequential. The evidence supports a long-lived legal relevance for the adoption, but not uninterrupted cooperation between the original parties throughout the intervening decades. It also cannot establish every death date, the precise timing of all earlier distributions, or all changes to the estate. A detailed published genealogy is a research hypothesis to test, not a substitute for those missing links.

#### C. The later division: one act, several witnesses

The principal later act is **TMH 10, 18a**, with parallel **BE 6/2, 23 = TMH 10, 18b**, and the joined envelope **18c**. Its date is **Samsu-iluna 4/10/21**: the “21” is a **day**, not the regnal year. The parallel tablets and envelope must not be counted as three divisions. Földi’s joins and seal observations identify the envelope relationship; his translation corrections must be applied to the appropriate witness. [D14–D16; S04, p. 253; S03, p. 4 n. 14]

The division concerns **Narubtum, daughter of Migir-Enlil**, and **Ur-Pabilsaga, son of Ubārum**. Narāmtum is called Migir-Enlil’s mother. The earlier adoption supplies Ur-Pabilsaga’s position in the estate history; the wording does not require a new adoption between him and Narubtum. Whether every parent–child relationship in the wider genealogy was biological is a separate question. [D14, rev. 1–5 and seals; D12]

The allocated items are specific rather than a merely abstract statement of partnership:

The field on Ur-Pabilsaga’s side is linked to compensation for the enslaved man allocated to Narubtum. The document does not set out an equal division of every type of asset or establish that the two totals were equal by a known market valuation. It records separate, bounded allocations and a no-challenge agreement. The named enslaved persons are people whose legal allocation is being determined by others; the instrument provides little access to their own choices or continuing family relationships. [D14, obv. 6–18; rev. 5–7; D15]

A later division following the earlier adoption is therefore secure. Identity of every earlier and later parcel is not. Similar areas, recurring place designations, or the repeated name Taribum can invite comparison, but cannot independently prove unchanged property boundaries or continuous identity for each dependent over several decades. The initial list and later allocation must not be conflated into one inventory. [D12 versus D14–D15]

#### D. The contested “keeping together” interpretation

Van Wyk interprets the agreement through a hybrid adoption-and-division template and proposes a stronger arrangement of continued co-ownership and household preservation. This is a substantive interpretation worth testing, but **the separately allocated shares, adoption recital and mutual waiver alone do not demonstrate perpetual inalienability, a right of survivorship, or continued shared residence**. An older reading of the relevant estate clause as “one-third” also differs from the newer all-property reading; Van Wyk herself acknowledges that reading difference. [S03, pp. 3–4 and later discussion; D14–D15]

The disagreement is not resolved by declaring the older interpretation impossible. Additional documents could support an enduring arrangement. The point is that the stronger social and proprietary conclusions require additional premises, and those premises are not independently secured in this return. Narubtum’s religious status likewise must not be inferred from her name and then used as a fixed explanation of the transaction: unlike Narāmtum’s status in the earlier adoption, it is not explicit in this division. [D12; D14–D15; S03, discussion of Narubtum]

**Case B assessment.** The secure microhistory has a female property holder, an adopted successor retaining his earlier filiation, and a later estate division in which that adoption still matters. It also has a woman receiving a separately described allocation, and enslaved persons distributed with other estate resources. It demonstrates **durable legal consequences and selective redistribution**. It does not establish a single continuously co-resident household, a complete three-generation biological genealogy, or the subsequent fulfilment of every support obligation. [D12–D16; C-C02, C-C15–C-C17]

### 4.4 Judicial countercase — Ilī-u-Šamaš and Amurrum-šēmi

SAOC 44, 24 records a dispute concerning **Ilī-u-Šamaš**, originally adopted from **Mārti-erṣetim**, his mother, by **Amurrum-šēmi**. The surviving narrative refers to twenty years and to rejection of the paternal relationship. Judges instruct the adopter to remove him from heirship and establish a no-claim instrument. The exclusion extends to Ilī-u-Šamaš’s heirs. This is a **recorded legal termination**, not merely an untriggered threat in an adoption contract. [D18, obv. 1–16; obv. 17–rev. 3; revised text incorporating S07 collations]

The damaged passages do not provide a day-by-day history of support or work. Nor does the text establish the son’s age at adoption, a debt-driven motive, a precise absolute date, or where he lived afterwards. The proposed link with the loan ARN 81 was not independently verified. A later rupture of heirship need not mean that every memory, social relationship or economic connection disappeared. Conversely, legal continuity cannot be assumed simply because an adoptive relationship had previously lasted a long time. [D18–D19; S07, partial access]

### 4.5 Supplementary instruments: retained benefit and variation

**TIM 4, 13**, attributed to Nippur with uncertainty, records Nanna-amaḫ’s earlier provision for Ipiq-Enlil, a subsequent heirship arrangement, future support, and benefit reserved during Ipiq-Enlil’s lifetime. The important contrast is between **a right assigned through an instrument and the timing of its enjoyment**. This is a more specific warning against collapsing title into immediate practical control than a general analogy with modern property. It remains evidence for a contractual arrangement, not direct observation of its later operation. [D26, obv. 1–13; rev. 8–9]

TIM 4, 13 and 14 use **one-third mina** in repudiation clauses, while OECT 8, 21 uses **half a mina**. The texts differ in chronology, circumstances and certainty of provenance. Their contrast rules out treating the inspected sample as if every adoption used a single numerical sanction; it does not establish how penalties were actually enforced. TIM 4, 14’s difficult opening and seal relationships are not used to create an additional genealogy or female-adoptee case. [D26, rev. 1, 7; D27, obv. 9, 15; D09, rev. 3, 11]

### 4.6 Cross-case interpretation: persistence, redistribution and rupture

The recurrent historical processes are not alternatives from which one universal model can be chosen. **Prior commitments could survive the people who made them; the resources associated with those commitments could be split or exchanged; and a relationship’s legal consequences could be terminated.** An earlier gift may constrain later heirs, while a later payment resolves a claim rather than preserving the original asset configuration. This coexistence is the central answer to the task. [D04, D07–D08, D14, D18]

Care provisions show that succession could be bound to continued material support, but the records do not warrant reducing all adoption to old-age insurance. Marriage-related heir appointment, property grants and litigation demonstrate other documentary settings. Nor do the male adoptees in this purposively selected corpus establish that girls could not be adopted. Explicitly enslaved persons are present in estate transmission, but that is not evidence that those same people became adopted heirs. The latter issue remains open pending direct Nippur texts. [D01, D05, D09, D12, D14; S12, abstract only]

Estate division did not concern only fields and houses. Recurring institutional interests, movable equipment, silver, enslaved persons and expense claims could all matter. Yet absence of an asset from one inventory does not establish its absence from a family’s total resources. A tablet may settle one category of property or one dispute. Differences between inventories therefore cannot, without further controls, be read as demographic decline, a wealth curve, or the disappearance of a household. [D03–D04, D07–D08, D12, D14, D25]

Architecture remains a necessary independent test for claims about co-residence and physical fission. A boundary clause defines property relative to neighbours; it does not supply a floor plan. A share does not itself identify a new wall, and a document preserved with a property history need not have been composed for the last people who occupied the building in which it was deposited. This return consequently identifies **legal redistribution**, not a securely mapped sequence of residential subdivisions for Cases A or B. [Document provenance ledger; D03, D07, D11, D14; S08 access limitation]

The audit contains corrections of different kinds. Some are **published re-readings** adopted here, such as the date of MC 3, 51. Others are **observed discrepancies**, such as a father-name variant or conflicting metadata, not matters this agent has independently solved. Still others are **limits on inference**, such as keeping namesakes separate. The distinction prevents a source-critical note from sounding like a new authoritative tablet edition.

The chronology of Mannum’s estate is not a minor clerical issue: a date shift changes whether inheritance can plausibly precede acquisitions. Nevertheless, chronological order alone does not establish financing. Similarly, the day/year mistake in the Narubtum division affects a family chronology but does not decide every dispute about its legal purpose. Interpretive claims must be revised at the level actually affected by the correction. [D06–D07; D14–D15; S01, S03]

### 5.2 Access and the limits of independent verification

Full access was not obtained to Stone and Owen 1991, Goddeeris 2016, the complete Meinhold 2020 chapter or the architectural content of Stone 1987. The research did obtain several underlying texts and important later corrections; it therefore does not depend solely on summaries. But uninspected parts of those publications cannot be represented as checked. The source ledger records direct, partial, indirect, abstract-only and unread status separately. [S05–S08, S11–S12]

The available digital records are not uniform critical editions. Some translations follow earlier readings; some catalogue language or date fields disagree with the displayed transliteration or a later specialist correction. A page headed “excavated in Nippur” may still lack a locus or mark detailed provenience uncertain. Artifact photographs, where linked, were not newly collated for this return. These limitations are especially important for damaged kinship clauses and exact estate totals. [D06, D11–D12, D14, D26–D27]

### 5.3 Meaningful negative evidence

The following are **not demonstrated**, rather than disproved: performance of all stipulated support; continuous joint administration throughout gaps between documents; automatic transfer of all debts to all heirs; a universal entitlement of eldest sons; uniform provision for daughters; adopted enslaved heirs in the examined local sample; exact residential membership; and the causes of an apparent decrease in listed assets. The strongest negative conclusion is methodological: the available observations are insufficient for those propositions. [D01–D27; C-C20–C-C24]

The cases should not be averaged into a “normal Nippur household.” Their evidentiary value lies in documenting possible and consequential processes with recoverable participants and contexts. A properly delimited exceptional case can establish that a process occurred; it cannot establish how often it occurred or how typical its participants were.

## 6. Comparative evidence outside Old Babylonian Nippur

**No out-of-city or later-period legal rule is used to fill a Nippur gap.** No clause from the Laws of Hammurabi is substituted for the actual local transactions, and no Ur III, Middle Babylonian or Neo-Babylonian example is counted as an Old Babylonian Nippur act.

Van Wyk’s broader Old Babylonian typology is used only to raise questions about the relation between adoption, heirship and documentary form. Its cross-local categories do not establish which combinations were common in Nippur. The 2025 study of adopted enslaved persons is retained as a promising comparative/retrieval lead, but its detailed geographic sample was not verified and it supports no concrete Nippur claim here. [S10, partial reading; S12, abstract only]

TIM 4, 13–14 are treated separately because their **Nippur attribution is uncertain**, not because another city has been securely established for them. Their legal wording can clarify interpretive possibilities, but they carry less weight for a place-specific proposition than securely attributed local evidence. This is a provenance qualification, not a silent importation into the main sample. [D26–D27]

**Which direct Nippur texts document adopted daughters or adopted enslaved heirs, and under what circumstances?** How do their contractual capacities and subsequent outcomes differ from the male heirs in the present core? [S05–S06; S12 as a retrieval lead]

- **Relevance to project question:** Prevents a misdated causal history from becoming an accepted proposition about household continuity.
- **Design status:** NO DESIGN DECISION

### Candidate 4 — Prior gifts could constrain later estate settlements

- **Claim / proposition:** A lifetime gift to Nanna-meša’s daughter Nin-kuzu was acknowledged in a later settlement involving other family claimants, compensation and waiver. Local claim: C-C09.
- **Period:** Hammurabi 31, month 5; earlier gift recalled but not independently dated here.
- **Geography / archive / social context:** Nippur; earlier title/family material associated with the Mannum dossier.
- **Evidence type:** Primary legal settlement with retrospective recital.
- **Primary evidence:** OECT 8, 4 = MC 3, 25, P283650, Ashm 1926-0379, obv. 1–20 and rev. 1 (D04).
- **Secondary scholarship:** Meinhold 2015 (S01); Hunter 1930 and Stone/Owen 1991 through selected digital edition (S13/S05).
- **Interpretation(s):** Successors encountered rights already created during a predecessor’s life; division was not necessarily a distribution from an untouched estate.
- **Uncertainty / disagreement:** The date of the later waiver is not the date of the original gift or a securely known death date. Broader kinship interpretations differ.
- **Confidence + rationale:** HIGH for the recalled gift, compensation and renunciation recorded in this settlement.
- **Limits on generalization:** No universal daughter’s share, dowry rule, equality rule, or proof of residence follows.
- **Relevance to project question:** Shows how earlier acts remained consequential when parties and generations changed.
- **Design status:** NO DESIGN DECISION

### Candidate 5 — Inherited resources could change form through exchange

- **Claim / proposition:** Mannum exchanged inherited and preferential built-property rights for fractions of recurring institutional rights. Local claim: C-C11.
- **Period:** Samsu-iluna 10, month 3, day 23.
- **Geography / archive / social context:** Nippur; Mannum-mešu-liṣṣur/Munawwirum transaction.
- **Evidence type:** Primary exchange instrument.
- **Primary evidence:** OECT 8, 16 = MC 3, 30, P283643, Ashm 1926-0384, obv. 1–12 and rev. 1–4 (D07).
- **Secondary scholarship:** Meinhold 2015 (S01); Meinhold 2014 (S02).
- **Interpretation(s):** Continuity of a successor’s resource position did not require retention of the same physical property bundle.
- **Uncertainty / disagreement:** The text does not establish revenue received, personally performed service or residence. A seal-filiation discrepancy remains unresolved.
- **Confidence + rationale:** HIGH for the exchange and categories of rights explicitly recorded.
- **Limits on generalization:** A specific contractual transfer, not a model of the whole institutional economy or evidence of a house move.
- **Relevance to project question:** Documents transformation within succession rather than equating continuity with unchanged possessions.
- **Design status:** NO DESIGN DECISION

### Candidate 6 — Earlier expense claims could affect later heirs

- **Claim / proposition:** A settlement recognized Mannum’s earlier expenses for Ibbī-Enlil and transferred an institutional entitlement from the heirs. Local claim: C-C12.
- **Period:** Samsu-iluna 10, month 9, day 3.
- **Geography / archive / social context:** Nippur; Mannum and the named heirs of Ibbī-Enlil.
- **Evidence type:** Primary retrospective settlement referring to examination of an expense record.
- **Primary evidence:** OECT 8, 11 = MC 3, 31, P283642, Ashm 1926-0380 (D08); linked heir group in OECT 8, 19, P283646 (D25), with name variant retained.
- **Secondary scholarship:** Meinhold 2015 (S01), discussion of these transactions.
- **Interpretation(s):** An obligation or claim arising during an individual’s life could alter resources allocated by successors.
- **Uncertainty / disagreement:** Recognition of a specific claim does not prove all support was performed, explain its motive, or establish an adoption relationship. Name/seal variants are flagged.
- **Confidence + rationale:** HIGH for the recorded claim, examination and settlement; less certain for every cross-document family link.
- **Limits on generalization:** Not a universal rule that all personal expenses or debts passed to all heirs.

### Candidate 7 — An earlier adoption remained consequential in a later division

- **Claim / proposition:** Narāmtum’s adoption of Ur-Pabilsaga was invoked in a later estate division between him and Narubtum; the later instrument records separate allocations, including enslaved persons. Local claims: C-C15–C-C16.
- **Period:** Rīm-Sîn I 43 to Samsu-iluna 4, month 10, day 21.

- **Confidence + rationale:** HIGH for the linked endpoints, named allocations and retrospective adoption clause.

### Candidate 8 — Heirship could be terminated with future claims excluded

- **Claim / proposition:** Judges directed termination of Ilī-u-Šamaš’s heirship and a no-claim instrument extending exclusion to his heirs. Local claims: C-C18–C-C19.
- **Period:** Undated Old Babylonian record; absolute date unresolved in this return.
- **Geography / archive / social context:** Nippur; Ilī-u-Šamaš, his mother Mārti-erṣetim, and adopter Amurrum-šēmi; excavation number 3N-T0873.

- **Uncertainty / disagreement:** Narrative damaged; twenty years are reported, but no exact age at adoption is established. Debt motive and long-term compliance not independently verified.
- **Confidence + rationale:** HIGH for the judicial instruction and recorded scope of exclusion in the revised text.
- **Limits on generalization:** One court case, not an estimate of revocation frequency or proof that every other social relationship ended.
- **Relevance to project question:** Adds a documented rupture to evidence of continuity; avoids assuming adoption was irreversible.
- **Design status:** NO DESIGN DECISION

### Candidate 9 — Legal continuity is not itself evidence of continuous residence

- **Claim / proposition:** The two reconstructed dossiers establish successive relationships, claims and reallocations without independently establishing an unchanged co-resident group. Local claims: C-C20–C-C22.
- **Period:** The core Lipit-Enlil-to-Samsu-iluna regnal sequence represented in this return.
- **Geography / archive / social context:** Selected Nippur legal/prosopographic clusters; exact residential associations unverified.
- **Evidence type:** Source-critical synthesis of primary records and provenance/access audit.
- **Primary evidence:** The directly read records within D01–D18 and D25, with access distinctions retained in §9.2; no independently examined house-plan sequence.

- **Interpretation(s):** Legal succession, property boundaries and social co-residence require separate evidence; one does not logically prove the others.
- **Uncertainty / disagreement:** Shared residence or cooperation may have persisted. Additional excavation/archive evidence could support it; this return does not disprove it.
- **Confidence + rationale:** HIGH for the stated verification limit; MODERATE for the cross-case historical synthesis.
- **Limits on generalization:** Not a universal definition of household, a denial of physical continuity, or a conclusion about all Nippur archives.
- **Relevance to project question:** Keeps an evidentiary boundary visible before any canonical historical or design decision is made.
