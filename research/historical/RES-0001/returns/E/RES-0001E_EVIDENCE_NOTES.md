## B. Case-level observations

### B1. BE 51 — hire and kin mediation

The named worker is Idin-Ištar, son of Nabi-Šamaš; the contract hires him **from Nabi-Šamaš**, not simply from an unnamed owner. Lu-Ninsianna is the hiring party. The stated interval runs from month III day 21 to month XII day 30. The edition translates the rate as 80 qa of grain per month. These particulars are the basis of E-C01. [P1909, p. 43]

The following remain distinct: the worker, the mediating father, the employer, and the recipient of the payment. The first three are named; the fourth is not made unambiguous by the quoted clauses. The contract supplies neither a numerical age nor a job description. Poebel’s suggestion that payment probably occurred at the end of the term, and his evaluation of the amount as small, are not explicit terms of the ancient agreement. No total for the entire period is calculated because the calendrical/proration convention has not been established.

**Rejected inference:** “A free child took a low-paid job and received wages personally.” Every italicized social detail in such a reconstruction would exceed the stated evidence.

### B2. BE 28 — two filial positions and a support obligation

Ibkuša adopts Ea-tayar as heir. Ea-turam is distinguished as the son by his wife, and the text sets out corresponding property shares and annual supplies for the father. The support duty concerns grain, wool, and oil; failure has consequences for heirship. The report does not rely on the damaged earlier grain clause or its uncertain interpretation. [P1909, pp. 29–30]

**What is known:** the recognized kin relationships, inheritance arrangement, categories of support, and default consequence. **What is not:** the age of each participant, location of their dwellings, who produced the supplies, or whether they personally worked together. The provision is evidence of support due from kin, not a complete labor organization chart.

### B3. BE 24 — adoption and prospective sale

Ea-idinnam and Kuritum adopt Ili-idinnam and Ili-ummati as children and heirs. Property division recognizes an elder brother’s preference. The repudiation clause threatens forfeiture and sale on the adoptees’ side; the parents’ side has property and silver consequences. The clauses are conditional. [P1909, pp. 27–29]

**Rejected inference:** the parties were enslaved because a future sanction could result in sale. The creation of heirship and the contemplated punishment occupy different temporal/legal positions. No later enforcement record was matched to this case in the present investigation.

### B4. BE 23 — allocation does not identify occupation

The explicit allocations are preserved without adopting the editor’s whole genealogy:

| Recipient’s share | Named enslaved people in the edition | What is missing |
|---|---|---|
| Narubtum | Ali-aḫuša; Iškur-rīm-ilī | Their work, own family, dwelling, and later history |
| Ur-Pabilsaĝa | Dumqi-Ištar; Taribum | Their work, own family, dwelling, and later history |

These are names in a legal partition, not four anonymous social categories. A balancing component involving land does not provide a general market price for the people concerned. [P1909, pp. 20–22]

Van Wyk supplies a useful map of competing readings of the final clause, but the report does not turn that map into a settled family tree. Her references to Jena witnesses also need direct concordance control before they can be treated as duplicate editions or separate events. [VW2018, pp. 21–22]

**Rejected inference:** the enslaved people necessarily maintained the gardens assigned in the same settlement. That is a possible reconstruction, not a task expressly assigned to them in the consulted text.

### B5. BE 48 and BE 40 — a controlled comparison

BE 48 combines Awilia’s marriage to Naramtum, a legal filial relationship between her and his three sons, inheritance provisions, and maintenance. The report calls her their legally constituted mother rather than supplying an unrecorded birth mother’s biography. Its list of future divisible property includes enslaved persons but gives no named-worker inventory. [P1909, pp. 35–38]

The wife’s repudiation clause includes sale and an uncertain bodily-marking expression in the old translation. No claim about the exact mark or physical technique is made. BE 40 provides a direct counterexample to a uniform sale provision: its repudiation clauses specify financial consequences. [P1909, p. 35]

**Date audit:** P1909’s catalogue gives BE 40 as Samsu-iluna 13/I/28. SZ1976 no. 24 dates its case to 13/I/26. The report therefore retains the month but does not settle the day. Neither the comparison nor the chronology of the other cases depends on that difference.

### B6. BE 43 — economic rights and institutional service

Igi-šag’s portion includes an Enlil priestly office for six months per year, associated support land, and other property. Sin-išmeanni is identified as his paternal uncle. The text’s time-defined right is the evidentiary anchor. [P1909, pp. 22–23]

Do not transform that six-month share into proof of six months of uninterrupted personal presence at the temple. No such attendance schedule was established. Nor does receiving a house share identify which rooms the recipient occupied. A kin/property agreement shows how rights were allocated, not every mechanism by which the associated service was performed.

### B7. BE 29 — a field, its cultivator, and its returns

Damu-idinnam leases Ibgatum’s field for cultivation with annual rent expressed as one-third of returns. This is a field-access/production-return agreement rather than a hire of a named person from another person. [P1909, pp. 43–44]

The document’s silence concerning helpers prevents classification of its labor force. It does not establish that the lessee personally did all the work, that the proprietor owned the lessee, or that either party lived on the field. No identifications with other people named Damu-idinnam were used to fill those gaps.

### B8. BE 8 — actual release, qualified place, unknown later life

The edition identifies Duššubtum as releasing Ištar-rabiat and records ten shekels paid to her. Forehead cleansing, a document, and protection against claims are present. The uncertain wording of a particular intervening formula is not translated anew. [P1909, pp. 38–39]

For geographic use, consult main section 5.1: this is **attributed Nippur**, not a secure residential find. Its content and its provenance are separate checks. The report neither treats the old Yokha label as final nor upgrades the recent reassignment into a known Nippur room.

**Kinship guard:** the heirs barred from making claims are identified through the former owner’s succession. Their presence cannot establish a family of the freed woman. **Capacity guard:** a recorded payment does not alone establish where the silver came from or a general right to accumulate independent assets. **Service guard:** clauses from a model contract are not missing portions of this tablet.

### B9. BE 57 — retained as a limited parallel

The agreement involving Tab-balatu, Beltia, and Ḫabil-aḫi combines adoption with property provisions and includes conditional consequences. Its catalogue date is Samsu-iluna 22/VI/16. Some physical-status terminology in the old rendering is uncertain; the report does not derive an additional occupational class or marking practice from it. [P1909, pp. 30–31, 152]

The broader inference sometimes made from such arrangements—that every adopted person was a child or every adoption was retirement provision—is not required by the clauses examined. A current edition and linked dossier would be needed for a fuller biography.

## E. Access accounting and reproducibility limits

**Actually used:** the task packet; selected P1909 primary-text editions and catalogue pages; SZ1976 catalogue and selected visual checks; current CDLI object entries; the relevant 2026 provenance chapter; accessible specialist discussions listed as directly consulted in the report.

**Not retrieved in full:** Stone 1987, Stone and Owen 1991, Goddeeris 2016, Spada 2018, and Goddeeris 2023. Their absence materially limits modern philological and residential verification. In particular, a modern author’s reference to another edition has not been relabeled “primary text consulted.”

**Not performed:** examination of original tablets; systematic handcopy/photo collation; a complete citywide legal corpus census; a comprehensive prosopography; a spatial reconstruction of individual laborers’ homes; a general dictionary study resolving every contested status term.

**How to reproduce a finding:** start with its local claim ID in the main evidence table, identify the named tablet and printed edition pages, read the ancient clauses separately from surrounding editorial interpretation, compare the object/date concordance above, and retain the listed unknowns. For E-C05, also reproduce the provenance check rather than relying solely on a current database label.

## F. Reconciliation handoff

The strongest candidates are the narrowly framed clauses and contrasts in E-C01–E-C08. E-C05 needs its geographic qualification attached wherever reused. E-C09 and E-C10 are useful controls on training and genre but do not establish service-contract biographies. No workstream has been asked to supply missing facts, and no conclusion depends on another task having finished.

The next evidentiary gains would come from direct modern edition access and a controlled match between texts, persons, and excavation records—not from widening the range of unsourced generalizations. The unresolved questions are specified in section 7 of the main report.
