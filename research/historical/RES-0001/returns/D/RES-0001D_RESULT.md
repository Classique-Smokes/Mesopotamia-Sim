### Principal answer

The strongest evidence does not identify a single, uniform form of “household ownership.” Instead, particular instruments connect named people through distinguishable claims: a transferable house share, a temporary cultivation right, an annual portion of a temple office, an entitlement to maintenance, a contingent restitution claim, or responsibility for a specified obligation. Kinship matters in many of these arrangements, but it does not remove the differences between their rights. [S01–S03; D-C01, D-C06, D-C08–D-C14]

Two conclusions are particularly well supported. **Legal division is not the same historical event as physical division or residential separation.** A partition can be followed by purchases that concentrate the resulting shares, or can retain common access. **An obligation, a conditional remedy, and a recorded performance are different evidentiary events.** Loan promises, inheritance-forfeiture clauses, receipts, and adjudicated conveyances must not be merged into one record of behavior. [BE 6/2, 32–35, 43, 65, 10; PBS 8/2, 116, 130; S01–S04]

The return does **not** establish citywide wealth distributions, normal interest rates, an automatic collective household liability rule, or the frequency and operation of collateral enforcement. It also does not identify residents from tablet findspots in fill or disturbed deposits. Those conclusions would require additional evidence, not stronger wording about the material already examined.

Separate CSVs preserve the full case inventory, evidence table, matrix, and source ledger for checking and reconciliation. Source identifiers S01–S15 resolve in section 9; case identifiers D-K01–D-K29 resolve in the case-inventory CSV. “Not established” means that this return does not establish a point, not that the ancient phenomenon did not exist.

## 2. Corpus and method actually used

### 2.1 What was examined

The case inventory contains **29 document/case groups**. Of these, **24 were examined in an edited text or digital edition**: 22 numbered texts in Poebel’s BE 6/2, and two CDLI editions of PBS 8/2 texts. This is examination of published readings and translations, **not independent cuneiform collation**. Two further cases, OECT 8, 4 and 11, are expressly mediated by Meinhold’s 2015 study. Three SAOC 44 loan cases are retained only as provisional leads from a search-indexed excerpt of Brisch 2017. Their inclusion does not make their primary editions directly consulted.

The 22 BE text numbers are **10, 11, 12, 15, 16, 21, 23, 26, 29, 32, 33, 34, 35, 39, 40, 42, 43, 44, 48, 51, 64, and 65**. They were selected for the task’s rights/obligations questions; they are not a random sample. Reading depth varies: some have full presented transliteration and translation, while the linked 32–35 sequence also relies on the edition’s discussion, catalogue, and later prosopographic correction. The two CDLI records are **PBS 8/2, 116 = CBS 07112 = P262151** and **PBS 8/2, 130 = CBS 07157 = P262191**. Their displayed texts and metadata were read in full. [S01–S03]

A document number is not always a separate transaction. PBS 8/2, 116 has a tablet and envelope recording one agreement. Likewise, parallel cases or envelopes in the BE material are not additional economic events. The rights matrix deliberately uses multiple rows for one instrument where it distinguishes different resources or rights. **Neither 29 case groups nor 37 matrix rows is an ancient population or transaction-frequency estimate.**

### 2.2 Source access and retrieval

Research combined searches by publication title, tablet number, museum/CDLI identifier, personal name, transaction genre, and scholarly bibliography. The main accessible primary edition was Poebel 1909, supplemented by the two CDLI records. Selected catalogue and text pages were checked visually, especially where tabular alignment or numerical clauses mattered. Quantities that remained uncertain were omitted rather than reconstructed from damaged OCR. Sumerian terminology was checked selectively against ePSD2; dictionary occurrences were not turned into transactions. [S01–S03, S14–S15]

Substantial selected text from Meinhold 2014 and 2015 was available through author-uploaded article transcriptions. These were used for corrections and controlled interpretation, not treated as substitute access to all their cited primary editions. Stone 1976’s introduction/catalogue was consulted, but its microfiche photographs were not collated. Stone 1987, Hunter 1930, and Stone/Owen 1991 were identified bibliographically but their complete primary materials were not obtained. Brisch 2017 was only partly accessible through metadata and selected indexed text. Spada/Hallo 2024 and Meinhold 2019 were consulted at abstract/description level. The source ledger specifies these limits individually. [S04–S13]

This was a targeted scholarly search, **not a systematic search with a demonstrably complete denominator**. No date-stamped export of every relevant CDLI record was obtained, no full library catalogue was exhausted, and no claim is made that all scholarship published by the completion date has been reviewed. Access failures were not counted as negative ancient evidence.

### 2.3 Chronology, provenance, and names

The directly examined dated core is principally late Rīm-Sîn, Hammurabi, and Samsu-iluna material. Earlier Isin/Larsa contexts enter mainly through provisional SAOC cases. It therefore does not sample the whole 1900–1600 BCE frame evenly. Dates are preserved as regnal years and months rather than forced into a single absolute chronology. **Si** means Samsu-iluna; **H** means Hammurabi; **RS** means Rīm-Sîn. Old catalogue designations such as **H 38b** are explicitly marked as Poebel’s labels, not silently converted into modern normalized dates. The CDLI day in PBS 8/2, 130 is uncertain. [S01–S03, S08]

“Nippur” and “excavated in a particular residential room” are different claims. The old edition’s site attribution usually does not provide a securely checkable house/locus association for the cases used here. **BE 6/2, 16 is Nippur(?) in Poebel’s catalogue** and remains qualified throughout. Names or transactions occurring elsewhere in the volume do not make the whole volume a Nippur corpus. BE 6/2, 8 is excluded from the core because Poebel assigns it to Yokha. [S01, introduction, catalogue p. 147, and pp. 38–39]

Personal names have been made readable without claiming comprehensive modern re-edition. Some remain in Poebel’s early reading, explicitly so for difficult names and office terminology. A repeated name does not establish an identical person: patronymic, date, role, associates, seals, and property description must support a linkage. The borrower Taribum is not equated with an enslaved person of the same name in another document. Different Nabi-Šamaš occurrences are not automatically joined into one creditor network. [S01–S05]

### 2.4 Evidence classification and confidence

The analysis distinguishes **a stated transfer or receipt**, **a promise of future performance**, **a conditional remedy**, **a retrospective narrative**, and **a scholarly reconstruction**. An executed legal instrument remains a legal representation: it can be stronger evidence of an event than a hypothetical clause without becoming an eyewitness account of every physical act.

Confidence attaches only to the proposition actually written. HIGH can characterize an explicit, well-supported contractual distinction or an acknowledged research boundary; it does not make a case representative. MODERATE flags consequential dependence on an older reading, a single reconstruction, or a mediated primary text. LOW marks the provisional indexed archaeological/institutional cases. Confidence in a conditional clause is not confidence that its condition occurred.

A person treated as property in an ancient allocation is identified as an enslaved person, without treating the document’s disposition as evidence of that person’s consent.
