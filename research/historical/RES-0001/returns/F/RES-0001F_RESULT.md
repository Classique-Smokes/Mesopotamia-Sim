## 2. Corpus and method actually used

### 2.1 Sources examined and what “examined” means

The central working source was Stone’s 1987 reanalysis, including its archaeological discussion, text catalog, and provenance concordances. Its searchable body was available through an author-hosted upload whose webpage title incorrectly identifies it as the 1976 contracts publication. The book’s own title page and the publisher’s metadata identify it unambiguously as *Nippur Neighborhoods*, SAOC 44. This report uses the book identity, not the webpage label. [S01]

Stone’s 1981 article was read in searchable form. The selected catalog pages of Stone’s 1976 OIMA 1 publication were examined as a PDF and visually checked. Robson’s 2001 article was read as a complete PDF; its published House F plan and fragment-join diagram were visually inspected. These are different access levels: reading an archaeological reanalysis does not amount to checking its underlying field notebooks, and inspecting a published photograph of a catalog page does not amount to reading the cuneiform tablet itself. [S02–S04]

Ancient texts in this return are used through published English catalog descriptions and specialist discussion. No new transliteration, translation, tablet-image collation, or independent identification of hands is claimed. Language and genre are not inferred from a document’s modern English description. Where the distinction between an operative document and a school exercise is unresolved, that uncertainty is preserved.

A contemporary bibliographic check located the 2023 *Back to School in Babylonia* volume and later work on Nippur’s chronology. Relevant chapter titles, publication details, and access restrictions were recorded. Unread chapters are not treated as corroboration, and their arguments have not been reconstructed from titles or promotional summaries. [S07–S09]

TA and TB are excavation areas within the broader Scribal Quarter, not three independent urban samples. House letters repeat between areas and periods. In particular, **TA House F is not TB House F**, and **TB 75 E-2 means locus 75, level E-2 in House O—not “House E.”** [S01, contents, pp. 29–35, 75–77, 91; S04, fig. 1]

The principal transactional sequence is eighteenth-century. Older material in the corpus extends back into the nineteenth century and, in some regnal ranges, earlier than the approximate project start. Such objects are retained as evidence of an assemblage’s history, not silently moved into the project’s period. The later seventeenth-century part of the approved frame is not comparably represented by the cases examined.

### 2.3 Method and confidence

Each association was tested at three levels: **where the object was found; which property or activity its content describes; and what connection this establishes to an identifiable person.** Only then was a residential interpretation considered. A legal sequence and a physical sequence were kept separate wherever their matching depended on assumptions.

Confidence follows the packet’s HIGH / MODERATE / LOW scheme. Most catalog-based historical claims remain MODERATE because the tablets were not collated and the archaeological associations are uneven. A HIGH rating for teaching activity does not extend to the teacher’s identity, number of pupils, or inhabitants. A LOW rating for a proposed family configuration does not imply that such a configuration was historically impossible.

The main biases are differential survival and excavation, selected object registration, later disturbance, incomplete buildings, the accumulation of old deeds, fragment counts that are not independent tablet counts, and recurrent publication of the same underlying evidence.
