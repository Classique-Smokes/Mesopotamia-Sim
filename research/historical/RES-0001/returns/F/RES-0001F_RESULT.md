### 2.3 Analytical procedure

The procedure was deliberately conservative. I first separated the **archaeological unit** from the **legal object** and the **named persons**. I then asked which links are direct: excavated location, explicit property description, named transaction, kin term, archive cache, seal, witness, or later reconstruction. Finally, I checked whether the proposed social conclusion requires a further assumption: co-residence, persistence across rebuilding, biological kinship, economic dependence, or a one-to-one mapping from a legal share onto rooms.

A text is therefore not treated as a resident list merely because it was excavated in a house. A building is not treated as a single timeless social unit merely because field plans use one house label. A legal transaction involving a house does not establish that the parties lived there. An archive associated with one person does not automatically identify the ownership of the excavated building in which it was found. Those distinctions govern the confidence assessments below.

### 2.4 Counting and selection limits

No total number of tablets, houses, or persons was used as a population denominator. The named cases were selected because they clarify different inferential problems: property sequence, archive association, school activity, subdivision, inherited document, lease, or sequential partition. Repeated mentions of the same transaction in catalog, article, and monograph were not counted as independent observations. A tablet and its case/envelope are treated as one legal episode unless the publication establishes otherwise.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 House I: strong property sequence, weak resident census

The House I case is the most tempting place to turn a sequence of property documents into a social narrative. NN 42–47 do form a coherent chain of allocations and transactions. Yet the strength of that chain lies in **property relations**, not in a list of occupants. The documents name people who hold or transfer shares; the archaeology supplies rooms and rebuilding; the mapping between those two domains is partly reconstructed.

Stone’s revised sequence places the relevant legal history across XI/XB/XA, while the catalog records the final find contexts of the principal texts in XA-1. This is analytically useful: it may indicate retained deeds, redeposition, or later archival storage. It also means the earlier architectural phases are not independently proven merely by the final deposit of the documents. The same text chronology used to build the phase model should not then be counted as a second independent line of evidence confirming it. [S01, pp. 65–67]

The 20-versus-40-gin discrepancy in NN 44 is a concrete example of why philological precision matters for archaeological reconstruction. Charpin’s indexed correction to 40 gin would make the subsequent 40 + 10 = 50 sequence internally consistent. But even if the correction is accepted after collation, the arithmetic identifies legal shares more securely than room boundaries. A 40-gin legal share is not automatically the set of rooms one later plan colors as one unit.

### 4.2 House F: activity and continuity are different questions

TA House F provides some of the best evidence in the corpus for **what happened in a building**. Robson’s reanalysis of exercise tablets, their reuse, joins across rooms, and associated installations supports a teaching environment. This is much stronger than an attempt to name all the inhabitants. The archaeological argument is about patterns of use, deposition, and movement of tablets within the house.

The later legal group NN 11–17 is therefore not a shortcut to the identity of the teacher or pupils. It belongs to a later deposit context and contains texts of different dates and legal subjects. The building can persist materially while its occupants, owners, users, or archival habits change. Rebuilding or reuse should be represented historically as a question to solve, not as proof of personal continuity.

### 4.3 Archive deposits: Atta and the limits of archive ownership

The Atta cache in TB House O is stronger than many other text–person links because a substantial group of documents centers on Atta’s transactions in temple-office rights. Yet the archive’s content is precisely why it cannot simply be relabeled “Atta’s house title archive.” The documents illuminate Atta’s economic/institutional relationships more directly than they illuminate ownership of the excavated building.

A person-centered archive, a building deposit, and a property title are different things. Their overlap is historically important when demonstrated. Their non-equivalence is equally important for avoiding overclaiming.

### 4.4 Subdivision and continuity without scripted fission

TB House C/C-1/C-2 shows a material transformation into smaller spatial units. That is real archaeological evidence of reorganization. What is not established here is the social mechanism: inheritance, sale, tenancy, partition, demographic change, or practical remodeling could all produce similar architecture. The absence of a matching deed is not proof of one mechanism over another.

This makes the archaeological sequence useful for testing social models without forcing a narrative. A simulation may later need to reproduce material consequences of household change, but this historical evidence does not authorize one deterministic cause.

### 4.5 Neighborhoods as networks of attested relations

Boundary clauses and repeated transactions support particular links among named persons and properties. They can establish adjacency or economic connection at a specified time. They do not automatically establish kinship, friendship, co-residence, or enduring community membership.

The safer reconstruction is therefore a network of separately typed edges: transaction, boundary, kin term, witness relationship, archive co-occurrence, and excavated spatial proximity. Some of these edges may converge on the same historical social group. The convergence must be demonstrated rather than assumed from a neighborhood label.

## 5. Disagreement, ambiguity, and source limitations

### 5.1 House I’s area discrepancy

The accessible 1987 catalog reports NN 44 as 20 gin, while an indexed passage of Charpin’s 1989 review says the correct reading is 40 gin. The 40-gin value creates a more coherent sequence with later 10- and 50-gin transactions. That coherence is evidence in favor of checking the correction, not a substitute for checking the tablet or complete philological argument.

### 5.2 Architectural phasing and document chronology

Stone’s revised phasing is sophisticated precisely because it uses documents, walls, and stratigraphy together. For present purposes, this means those observations are not statistically independent. Any later synthesis should record which component of a reconstruction came from physical stratigraphy and which from a dated legal text.

### 5.3 Original excavation access

OIP 78 and field records were not independently read in full during this task. Statements about original wall lines, tombs, installations, and exact find locations are therefore mediated through later publications unless otherwise noted. This does not invalidate them, but the access level matters for confidence.

### 5.4 Later scholarship not yet integrated

The 2023 *Back to School in Babylonia* volume contains directly relevant chapters on House F and its environment, economic life, and neighborhood archaeology. Those chapters were located but not read because the accessible PDF exceeded the tool’s retrieval limits. They remain a priority for follow-up rather than a source of implicit corroboration.

### 5.5 Chronological endpoint

Later work on Samsu-iluna and Nippur’s reconquest/abandonment was identified but not sufficiently read to revise the project’s broader chronology. The residential evidence in this return should not be used to define a single citywide abandonment event.

## 6. Comparative evidence outside Old Babylonian Nippur

This workstream did not use external cities or later periods to fill missing Nippur household facts. Comparative evidence appears only in the source criticism of methods, particularly the use of ethnographic analogy in older residential scholarship. Such analogies may generate questions about how rooms were used or how property division can affect architecture, but they do not identify Nippur residents or prove a household form.

The OIMA 1 sequential-partition examples are Nippur legal controls but lack the secure stratified house context of the central archaeological cases. They therefore serve as an internal comparison between a legal sequence with weak spatial resolution and a building sequence with stronger spatial data.
