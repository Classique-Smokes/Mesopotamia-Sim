## 2. Corpus and method actually used

### Actual reading, not a claim to comprehensive access

The work used a **purposive, contrastive sample**: cases were selected to test the differences between secure context and inferred provenance, explicit and reconstructed dates, functional and didactic writing, individual witnesses and composites, and original publication and subsequent correction. It was not randomly sampled. No full CDLI export, site-wide inventory, unpublished museum archive, or complete excavation register was available for counting.

Primary evidence was approached through published editions and catalogue records, not physical examination. Seven individual CDLI records were inspected: **P256212, P503174, P453321, P255088, P512610, P368911 and P265460**. Six were accessible as record pages; P368911 was accessible through indexed record text rather than a successful direct fetch. This count is an audit of retrieval, not seven independent historical events. Two additional inheritance fragments were read in Peterson’s edition, and two market tablets through Kreitzscheck’s edition and Béranger’s correction [S09, S14–S15, S18–S24].

Selected original text from Stone’s archaeological synthesis, Meinhold’s archive reanalysis and Béranger’s late-period study was read through author-hosted HTML. Robson’s author manuscript was read in selected PDF pages, including an inspected screenshot. The NABU correction was read in parsed PDF prose; screenshot retrieval failed. A contemporary excavation account was also read. These are genuine readings of the named material, but **not cover-to-cover readings of every book or independent checks of all their graphics** [S01, S04, S07–S09, S12, S15].

The full 1976 contract edition, OIP 78 excavation report, TMH 10 administrative edition, 1991 adoption/archive monograph and several major articles were not retrieved in full. Their publisher descriptions, abstracts or later discussions are labeled as such. They do not count as independently verified corroboration [S02–S03, S05–S06, S10–S11, S13, S25–S29].

### Chronological and contextual reach

The record-level sample includes regnal anchors under Sumu-el, Iter-pisha and Samsu-iluna, as well as undated or broadly dated objects. The longer reach into the late Old Babylonian period comes principally from specialist discussion of external cultic/administrative evidence, not a newly collated sequence of residential archives. CH01–CH07 make this difference explicit. Absolute BCE values are retained only where the cited scholarship supplies them; the late chronology uses that scholarship’s Middle Chronology convention [S12, S18–S23].

Excavation contexts actually assessed in scholarship include TA/TB, House K, House O and TA House F. Other house labels are provided as retrieval leads, not newly assessed households. The reconstructed Mannum-mešu-liṣṣur dossier and the Dūr-Abi-ešuh material have different evidentiary statuses from excavated room deposits. Neither is silently assigned a Nippur house [S01, S07–S09, S12, S14–S15].

### Method and confidence

For each consequential claim, I separated the surviving object or edition, the editor’s reading, the proposed identification/context, and the further social inference. Names and legal terms were not newly translated into household categories. Missing or restored signs, missing dates, provisional provenance and indirect access remain visible. HIGH confidence concerns the **narrow proposition stated**, including some clear methodological distinctions; it does not make an archive representative of the city.

The sample disproportionately contains material that is published, searchable, name-rich or important in existing debates. Accessible legal and educational examples outnumber directly examined administrative or epistolary series. This selection makes negative claims about the less visible especially unsafe. A person who cannot be recovered here may be absent because no relevant act was written, because the record was not retained, because it did not survive, because its context was lost, or because it was not retrieved in this research. The return cannot distinguish those explanations without additional evidence.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 What is actually recoverable?

The best-supported unit of social observation in this return is a **specified attestation in a specified document**, sometimes strengthened by other dated texts or an archaeological association. A document can establish that a person was named as a party, relative, witness, neighbor, official, or object of transfer in its recorded context. That is already useful evidence. It is not automatically a biography, an exhaustive list of associates, or proof that everyone mentioned lived together [A-C02, A-C09, A-C22].

A reconstruction becomes stronger when its independent supports answer the same question. A dated filiational statement can help identify a person across texts; an excavated deposit can help locate document custody; a property boundary can connect adjacent holdings. But these observations are not interchangeable. Agreement about ownership does not alone establish residence, and agreement about residence does not alone establish the kind or duration of a relationship. The appropriate conclusion is the intersection actually supported, rather than the fullest household story compatible with it [A-C04, A-C07–A-C08].

This distinction also preserves meaningful positive evidence. The inheritance example TX03 does not become unusable merely because it is unrepresentative. It supports a narrow proposition about the named participant and transaction. The error would be either to inflate that example into a universal rule or to erase it because a citywide rate cannot be calculated [S20; A-C09].

### 4.2 The archaeological map is not a demographic map

HC01–HC05 show why text–building associations must be described at their actual resolution. A secure find in a building is valuable, but the tablet’s date, the building’s phase, the deposit’s formation, and the person’s relationship to the document can differ. In principle, a document might concern property elsewhere, preserve an earlier title, or enter a deposit through reuse. Each possibility requires evaluation in the particular context rather than being assumed universally [S01, S07; A-C03–A-C05].

The practical consequence for historical interpretation is to distinguish **what is associated** from **why it is associated**. A room with many documents is not self-interpreting. The explanation “household archive” needs the content and depositional argument that connect those documents to a social group. Conversely, a secondary deposit does not make the writing historically worthless; it weakens some spatial inferences while leaving its textual evidence available. These are limits on inference, not classifications of whole genres as reliable or unreliable.

### 4.3 Reconstructed archives and person-linking

The Mannum-mešu-liṣṣur dossier is particularly useful because scholarly grouping, document ownership and genealogy do not coincide automatically. Meinhold’s reassessment differentiates documents addressed to the archive owner, earlier title material, uncertain inclusions and a document not owned by him; it also revisits filiations using seal evidence. In particular, the seals identify Nanna-meša and Adda-kalla as sons of Lu-Bau, contrary to the earlier reconstruction of them as Lu-Bau’s adoptive brothers [S08, pp. 8–10]. The exact inclusions matter before any social interpretation of the dossier’s apparent size or continuity.

A separate small case makes the inferential chain unusually clear: **UM 29-16-672** lacks its date. Peterson proposes early Samsu-iluna on combined prosopographic and scribal evidence and suggests, rather than establishes, an archive connection. The other fragment, **UM 29-16-191**, preserves little identifying information beyond an elder-brother designation [S09, introduction and §§2.1–2.2]. Those are different levels of recoverability, even though both documents can contribute to material and legal history.

A defensible identification therefore needs more than a matching name. Relevant support can include compatible dates, a securely read parent, an office or seal, co-occurring parties, or a property history. Contradictions must remain active constraints. Reconstructing a date from an identification and then treating that date as independent proof of the same identification would be circular. The same caution applies when archive membership is both the premise and conclusion of a family reconstruction [A-C07–A-C08, A-C20].

### 4.4 Legal, institutional and educational evidence answer different questions

A legal document’s strength is its formal statement of the particular act, claim, obligation or relationship at issue. It does not necessarily describe all arrangements that surrounded that act. An administrative record answers the needs of the recording operation and can expose people in different capacities. The important Jena corpus is therefore not dispensable merely because the accessible private documents are socially rich; however, its detailed contribution has not been independently established in this return [S10; A-C16].

Didactic writing is different again. TX06 demonstrates the problem with treating a broad catalogue label as sufficient evidence of an actual transaction. Conversely, the absence of a date, witnesses or a seal cannot by itself prove that a damaged document was an exercise. Genre identification should use the complete available combination of format, contents, context and edition, not a single negative feature [S16–S17, S23; A-C10].

Lexical and literary sources can be valuable controls on vocabulary and scribal categories. Peterson’s commentary provides examples of lexical comparison in interpreting an inheritance inventory; TX07 shows the distinction between an individual witness and a reconstructed literary composition. Neither use licenses counting a lexeme or literary character as an independently observed household member [S09, S24; A-C18].

### 4.5 Late Nippur: neither a blanket cutoff nor unbroken residence

Béranger proposes complete abandonment between **Ammiṣaduqa 16 and 18, approximately 1631–1629 BCE**, using, among other evidence, **TMH NF V, 77 = HS 189** and cultic receipts. The proposal challenges an earlier blanket cessation but does not show that the excavated residential sample continued unchanged. The relocation interpretation associated with Charpin is known here through Béranger, not a direct reading of Charpin’s chapter [S12, pp. 30–33; S13, indirect].

The distinction to preserve is between a text referring to Nippur, activity occurring there, an institution retaining its identity elsewhere, and a particular neighborhood remaining occupied. LC01 records the relevant sources without merging those propositions. No historical reason permits a late receipt by itself to extend the occupation date of every earlier house [A-C14].

### 4.6 Social visibility and the danger of a denominator

The sample can reveal relationships across legal, property, institutional and educational settings; it does not reveal every participant in those social settings equally. People named as principals receive a different kind of representation from people listed among transferred assets, relatives used for identification, or workers named for a limited purpose. The presence of a name can establish visibility without giving the person an independently preserved voice [S09, S20; A-C09, A-C19].

There is no defensible denominator here for women, children, enslaved persons, dependents, property holders, scribes, migrants, or residents. Counting surviving names would mix repeated attestations, uncertain identifications, duplicate witnesses, different periods and genre-specific inclusion. Even a carefully deduplicated list would still be a list of documented persons in a defined corpus, not a census of the population that produced it. The social-visibility matrix in the companion map states what each observed role can and cannot support [A-C11–A-C12, A-C19].

### 5.2 Limits of the present research

**No independent epigraphic collation.** The return uses existing transliterations and translations. It does not certify every restoration, sign reading, seal attribution or kin term. An editorial translation and a surviving sign are not the same level of evidence.

**No independent complete stratigraphic reanalysis.** The report identifies important house/context routes but does not redraw the plans, verify every level correlation, assess all burials, or calculate household capacity from architecture. The source map cannot replace Task F’s detailed archaeological work.

**No complete archival census.** Administrative, letter and unpublished holdings remain unevenly represented. The corpus descriptions in S02 and S10 are not substituted for direct examination of their full contents.

**No automatic preference for recency.** A later study can introduce a correction, reinterpret an unchanged witness, or inherit an earlier error. A genuine new collation may strengthen a reading; a reprint alone does not. The actual witness and correction history matter more than how many citations repeat a conclusion [S08; A-C20].

**No unqualified negative social findings.** Failure to find a practice or person in this purposive sample is not evidence that they were absent from Old Babylonian Nippur. A stronger negative argument would need a defined class of records in which the item would ordinarily be expected, adequate survival/access, and an assessment of alternative reasons for silence.

## 6. Comparative evidence outside Old Babylonian Nippur

### 6.1 Proposed Larsa sphere: Cotsen 52175 / Education 78

Spada’s two model contracts are unprovenienced; the proposed Larsa connection rests on internal features, not an excavated Nippur context. They are useful comparative evidence that plausible legal wording and named participants can occur in exercises. They are **not direct evidence of Nippur adoption behavior, local transaction frequencies or household composition** [S16].

### 6.2 Dūr-Abi-ešuh and other proposed origins

Material attributed to Dūr-Abi-ešuh may bear on Nippur’s institutions and displaced people while remaining geographically external and archaeologically insecure. That relevance does not erase the distinction between ancient institutional affiliation, writing location, destination and findspot. LC01–LC02 retain both the Nippur connection and the attribution problems [S12, S14–S15].

### 6.3 Lexical parallels from other places or periods

The inheritance commentary invokes comparative lexical evidence, sometimes from later traditions. Such evidence may support a reading or expose semantic alternatives. Its date and place cannot be stripped away and its objects or practices counted in the Nippur sample [S09; A-C18].

### 6.4 Later Babylonia and ethnographic analogy

Prosobab’s **620–330 BCE** remit is far outside the approved period; its people and social patterns were not imported [S30]. Ethnographic analogy is an interpretive method associated with the residential scholarship, not a direct witness to Old Babylonian social organization [S05, bibliographic scope]. Any analogy must face the local evidence rather than supply missing family members or a default household type.
