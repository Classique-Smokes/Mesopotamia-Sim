## 2. Corpus and method actually used

### 2.1 What was read, and what was not

The core consists of **eight legal documents** read in Poebel’s primary edition: **BE 6/2 10, 36, 37, 39, 42, 45, 57 and 66**. This means consultation of available transliterations, translations and associated commentary, not a fresh reading of original tablets. The relevant printed passages are pp. 5–6, 11–17, 30–31 and 45–48. The introduction, selected catalogue entries and additional control passages were also checked. Poebel’s book is an old edition: its exact readings, English functional glosses, metrological renderings and general legal theories must not be accorded the same evidentiary status. [S01]

Four further legal records—**OBCN I 19, 28, 29 and 32**—were read only through Stone’s catalogue. Nos. 28–29 form one linked case row, not two independent examples of office inheritance. Three administrative records—**TMH 10 165, CBS7627 and CBS7580**—were sampled through the edition/table entries in Goddeeris 2022. Thus the interface table has **14 case rows representing 15 distinct text identifiers**. This is an inventory of this return, not a statistical sample of the ancient population. [S02–03]

Access tiers are kept separate: **edited primary text**, **catalogue synopsis**, **documentary table mediated by a study**, **scholarly interpretation**, and **metadata/abstract only**. An abstract did not authorize claims about an unseen tablet. In particular, Meinhold 2019 was verified but not obtained in full; Stone 1987 was not read as a book; and Goddeeris 2016 and Stone–Owen 1991 remain re-edition routes rather than fully consulted corpora. [S04, S06–09]

### 2.2 Selection, provenance and identification

The selection was purposive: transactions mentioning temple offices, institutional titles, office/inheritance deeds or institutional allocations were prioritized. Unrelated property and labor documents were used as controls against overclassification. There was no random selection and no count of all surviving Nippur households.

Poebel expressly distinguishes his Nippur material from other provenances in the volume. This return does not treat the book’s title, “chiefly from Nippur,” as a blanket provenance statement. Nos. 8–9 and the later non-Nippur appendix were not absorbed into the core. The conflicting provenance notes for nos. 6 and 47 were another reason not to use them as positive cases. A separately checked CDLI record, P258016, demonstrates the danger: its BE 6/2 reference concerns a non-Nippur record. [S01, pp. 1–2 and catalogue; S11]

No main case has a securely verified individual house/locus assignment in this research pass. Publication provenance, a reconstructed dossier, explicit kinship and archaeological co-residence are different things. A patronymic helps identify a person; it does not establish a shared dwelling. Final page-image checks of Poebel’s catalogue place nos. 36, 37, 39, 42, 45 and 57 respectively in Samsuiluna 11/9/28, 11/11/6, 12/5/15, 13/12, 14/12 and 22/6/16; unspecified days remain unspecified. These are the published catalogue dates, not new readings of the year names. [S01, pp. 149–152] A plausible document linkage is kept as a hypothesis until its dates, parties, titles and asset descriptions have been compared.

### 2.3 Reading and confidence rules

For each case the report separates: what was transferred; who is named; the stated family connection; the institution or title; the resource or consideration; an obligation actually written; and anything left unstated. A **price paid to acquire a right**, a **resource allocated for a purpose**, and **income available for household consumption** are not substitutes for one another. Neither is the enumeration of an office proof that the named holder personally completed all of its possible duties.

**HIGH** applies to narrow propositions with converging primary support or particularly clear contextual support. **MODERATE** is used where a single case, an old reading, a synopsis, or an uncertain relationship materially limits confidence. **LOW** marks a proposed linkage or reconstruction needing verification. A mixed-confidence row explicitly identifies which component is weaker. The method/confidence restrictions in G-C13–14 concern this return, not a discovered ancient rule. [S16, evidence protocol]

Personal names are retrieval labels, not an independent philological contribution. In particular, Poebel’s Nin-IB is rendered Ninurta for readability, while uncertain readings and office expressions remain visible. No modern square-metre or acreage conversions are imposed on damaged or ambiguously parsed area figures. Published ancient units and regnal dates are retained where safely recoverable.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Office, entitlement and service must be kept distinguishable

BE 6/2 36 provides an unusually useful entry point because it brings several evidentiary elements together. Ellumūšu, identified as a son of Ṣilli-Šamaš, conveys rights connected to the Martu temple and house property to Ilī-iddinam, likewise identified by that patronymic. The temple portion lists six office designations and specifies two months and twenty days in the year. The document characterizes the seller’s office/house interest as inherited and states a composite silver consideration. A clause bars future claims by the seller and his heirs. [S01, pp. 5–6, no. 36; G-I01]

These are several observations, not one automatic social conclusion. An annual period shows how the conveyed entitlement is delimited. It does not show that the person continuously worked in the temple for that period, personally performed six distinct jobs, or lived in an institutionally maintained residence. Nor does purchase of house property alongside the right identify that house as the purchaser’s or seller’s place of residence at the date of the document.

The interpretation does not depend on preserving Poebel’s English labels for each profession. His rendering includes a temple superintendent, caterer, doorkeeper, courtyard cleaner and uncertain vessel-related function alongside the pašīšu office. The underlying listed designations are more secure for the present question than a reconstructed modern job description. The report therefore treats this as an **office/right bundle**, not six verified occupations of one worker. [S01, no. 36, and discussion surrounding no. 39]

Kusu provides a second primary example of a recurring annual period. BE 6/2 39 identifies two months per year and an exchange rather than a simple cash purchase. Together, the two texts justify the narrow claim that some temple-linked rights were divisible or describable in annual periods. They do not supply a citywide rota or the qualifications required to exercise each role. [S01, pp. 15–16, no. 39; G-C01]

### 4.2 Transfers connect institutional rights to other assets, not to a modern profit calculation

The transfer evidence supports more than hereditary succession. In no. 36, inherited rights can subsequently be sold. In no. 39, Ilušu-bāni transfers a Kusu-related package to Enlil-lušag and receives a field together with five shekels of balancing silver. The stated rationale is that the field is not equal in value to the office interest. No. 37 similarly includes Nuska offices and fields in an exchange with a two-shekel adjustment. These are direct observations of legally expressed conversion between different kinds of resources. [S01, nos. 36, 37, 39; G-I01–03]

They do not establish a single, freely accessible market in all offices. No text in this selected set enumerates the entire class of eligible buyers or provides a universal statement that temple authorization was unnecessary. A transaction occurring between named persons does not settle every institutional constraint that might have governed it. Conversely, the institution’s absence as a named seller in a particular conveyance does not mean it had no interest in the service or entitlement.

The quantities invite misleading calculations. Forty-three shekels in no. 36 concern a **combined** office/house transaction. Treating that sum as the price of the temple right alone discards one of the stated assets. The five and two shekels in nos. 39 and 37 are **adjustments**, not the full prices of the offices, and not periodic distributions. The annual term gives a duration of entitlement, not its annual produce value. Dividing any of these silver amounts by the number of days would generate a number, but not a defensible wage or investment yield. [S01, specified payment clauses; G-C04, G-C10]

This distinction is consequential for household history. The evidence shows capacity to mobilize or exchange assets in a particular transaction. It does not show whether the purchaser paid from savings, borrowed, received assistance, expected subsistence support, or pursued a financial investment. Those questions require other documents belonging to the same verified people and rights. The full price/remuneration discussion in Meinhold 2019 is an important missing check, not a substitute for it. [S04, abstract and access limitation]

### 4.3 Inheritance and alienability are compatible; continuity may involve recovery

No. 36’s inherited status and subsequent sale rule out a false opposition between “family property” and “transferable property” for that particular right. The same resource can have both descriptions at different points in its history. A family connection is therefore best understood here as a documented relation to an asset, not as an assumption that the asset could never leave the lineage. [S01, no. 36]

BE 6/2 66 adds a different route: Ninurta-rahim-šerim’s acquisition is explicitly characterized using the office of the paternal house. The document names Enki and Damgalnunna in the transfer and records silver consideration. The narrow historical point is the combination of a **family recovery claim** with a **particular acquisition**, not an unlimited hereditary title that extinguished other interests. Its exact legal mechanism needs a modern check, including how the divine party designations and the damaged closing clause should be understood. [S01, pp. 13–15, no. 66; G-I04]

A field transaction supplies an independent comparison within Nippur. In no. 45, Lamazum’s recovery is tied explicitly to the field of her paternal house, after an earlier alienation described in the instrument. This makes continuity a history capable of interruption and attempted restoration. The motive cannot be filled in: recovery might have had economic, familial, social or cultic significance, but the deed does not rank these possibilities. [S01, pp. 11–12, no. 45; G-I07]

Poebel discussed these transactions using “redemption” language and drew on the Biblical book of Ruth when considering the legal limits of recovery. That analogy is not primary evidence for Nippur. This return does not infer a compulsory right to force a resale, a fixed redemption window, or a universal privilege possessed by every paternal relative. It preserves the recoveries that the documents record while leaving the wider rule unsettled. [S01, pp. 14–15; G-C05]

### 4.4 Adoption could protect differences within a family, not merely merge property

BE 6/2 57 is important precisely because a broad “adoption creates a household” summary would lose its most informative detail. Tab-balatu and his wife Beltia adopt Habil-ahi. The instrument regulates division of the adoptive estate after the elder brother’s preference share, but also forbids that brother, Ninurta-gamil, from making claims against separately identified material associated with Habil-ahi: office-related rights and other property, together with a sonship document associated with Ablum, the gala-priest. [S01, pp. 30–31; G-I08]

The result is a distinction **within** a newly articulated family relationship. Adoption does not automatically erase the prior resource history of the adoptee. The deed’s treatment of the brother’s claims is direct evidence that a family arrangement could be constituted while preserving specific boundaries around institutional patrimony.

Two stronger reconstructions would outrun the available reading. First, the exact preceding relationship to Ablum should not be made certain without checking the modern edition: the old wording is not a sufficient basis for assigning every parent, inheritance and earlier adoption in a family tree. Second, nothing here demonstrates that the act of adoption itself conferred ritual competence, personally transferred cultic service or made the adoptee an active performer in a named temple. The office/property clause and the creation of kinship are explicit; the operational cultic result is not. [S01, no. 57; modern re-edition lead S09 p. 47, identified via S10]

This is still a substantive answer to the question about household continuity. Formal kinship could be relevant to preserving an institutional resource history, while the parties negotiated which resources were exposed to new sibling claims. It is a case-specific arrangement, not proof that this was the principal reason for adoption in Nippur. [G-C06]

### 4.5 Women’s institutional affiliation and family property: a narrow positive finding

BE 6/2 45 identifies Lamazum and Beltani with the same Ninurta-related female religious title, rendered here conservatively as **SAL-ISḪIB of Ninurta**. Lamazum acts in a transaction recovering a field characterized by its connection to her paternal house; the deed states a seven-shekel payment and the seller’s renunciation of future claims. Religious designation, a father’s identity, personal transaction and an ancestral-property assertion coexist in the same evidence. [S01, pp. 11–12; G-I07]

That combination is enough to reject an exclusively male description of every documented household–institution interface in this sample. It is not enough to say that this was a sale of a woman’s prebend. The transferred object is a field. Neither the religious title of the participants nor a divine element in a field name settles the field’s ultimate institutional ownership, its tax/service incidence or whether its produce remunerated cultic work.

The exact translation of the female title also remains a philological question. It is not replaced with a culturally loaded or mechanically specified role. The deed does not state a full schedule of duties, explain the participants’ religious careers, or provide a general rule for all similarly titled women. A modern edition and the full Meinhold article are needed before extending this finding to particular female-held prebends. [S01, no. 45; S04, access limitation]
