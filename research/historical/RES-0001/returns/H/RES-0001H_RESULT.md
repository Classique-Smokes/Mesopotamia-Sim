## 2. Corpus and method actually used

### Corpus actually examined

The principal corpus is **25 digital ancient-text records**: 22 editions in Stone’s *Nippur Neighborhoods* (SAOC 44), BE 6/2, 28, PBS 8/2, 116, and the didactic PBS 8/2, 173. These were read in CDLI’s displayed transliterations, with digital translations where available. The 24 functional/legal records are not all complete, nor equally decisive. Several serve as counterexamples or controls on interpretation rather than as foundations for broad claims. Q01 and L01 are additional audit records, not extra directly read legal texts.

The dated sample includes Sin-iddinam, Rim-Sin, Hammurapi, Samsuiluna and a record attributed to Ilima-ilum. It is concentrated within the commissioned Old Babylonian frame, **not evenly distributed over 1900–1600 BCE**. Regnal dates are retained rather than converted to a single absolute chronology without a dedicated chronology check. Texts with discrepant or broken dates remain so designated.

Archaeological associations are strongest at the catalogue level for **TA 184 / XA 1** (T06–T09), **TB 75 / E 2** (T22–T24), and **TB 200 / II 2** (T21). T11 preserves a catalogue distinction, X 1, rather than silently converting it to XA 1. Other objects have Nippur attribution but no independently established room in this return. These are textual-find associations, not asserted household rosters. Selected Stone 1987 discussion provides an additional check on the weakness of some house–party links (S01, pp. 58–59).

### Reading procedure and confidence

The unit of analysis is the **expression in its clause and document**, not an isolated dictionary lemma. For each record the register preserves identifier, attributed date, provenance, genre, observed written language, relevant passages, short forms, inference and limitation. Square brackets indicate restoration; question marks signal uncertainty. Subscripts are normalized for readability, but this is not a fresh sign-by-sign edition. Proper-name normalization is not used to merge people across archives.

“Direct” means that the digital text itself was consulted. It does **not** mean that the clay object, photograph, handcopy and all subsequent collations were independently reconciled. Modern interpretations mediated through another author are explicitly described as such. Model texts are not counted as functional transaction records; a tablet and its envelope, retired CDLI identifiers and repeated editions are not independent events.

HIGH, MODERATE and LOW apply to the proposition and context stated. A clear clause can support a HIGH-confidence narrow observation without justifying a citywide rule. Confidence in a word’s visible reading can differ from confidence in the proposed legal or social consequences.

### Coverage and access limits

House/property, affiliation, support and transferable temple rights have the strongest contextual coverage. Comprehensive labor classifications, the full semantics of particular offices, spoken bilingualism, and a systematic Sumerian–Akkadian lexical correspondence study remain less securely covered. No observed absence is treated as proof that a term, class or practice did not exist in Nippur.

The inspected sample selects people and assets visible in legal documentation, including titled women and temple-connected parties. It does not represent all inhabitants, all work, or the relative frequency of any social arrangement. Search access also favored CDLI and accessible editions. No frequency statistics or claim of exhaustive literature coverage is offered.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Premises, property, household and “family”

The immediate reading of a property term should follow its object description. A specified area, neighboring plots and doors or other fixtures identify a conveyance object. A parcel’s association with a person identifies another relation. An explicit residence clause supplies information that the association alone does not. The observed contrast between the rental T09, exchange T07 and residence wording T13 is therefore more useful than assigning one permanent English equivalent to é. [T07, T09, T13; H-C01–02.]

Nor is “estate” a neutral enlargement of “house.” A contents clause can distinguish movable property from the built plot, while the agreement’s other clauses identify what is actually allocated or withheld. “Family” may conveniently describe the named relationships in an analysis, but the examined sources do not warrant silently reading a bounded modern nuclear family, a corporate lineage, a co-residential unit and all heirs as the same thing. That last statement identifies an **inferential limit**, not a claim that Nippur lacked any such social formations. [T16–T19; H-C19–20.]

The temporal and archaeological questions must remain independent. Tablets found together can concern earlier transactions, different properties and people not demonstrably resident in the recovery location. Stone’s discussion of the nonspecific rental in the House F cluster is a concrete warning, not a reason to discard all archaeological context. [S01, pp. 58–59; T08–T09; H-C18.]

### 4.2 Affiliation and succession: what the instruments distinguish

In BE 6/2, 28, the father is expressly alive when shares are allocated; the annual provision and heirship consequence occur in the same instrument. “Inheritance” is usable as an explanatory translation only when it does not suppress that sequence. The brother terminology in the allocation likewise cannot be treated as independent proof of shared biological parents. [T01; H-C04–05.]

Other distinctions are not reducible to that example. In T02, spouse and parent/child relations occur together; its conditional sanctions describe possible legal responses, not subsequent biographies. In T16, elder-brother language qualifies a distribution, while a sale’s future-heir formula performs a different documentary function. These examples prevent both extremes: treating all kinship as unmediated biology, or treating every legal kinship expression as an empty fiction. [T02, T16–T19; H-C06, H-C19–20.]

The frequently used scholarly contrast between “real” and “fictional” adoption needs particular care. Van Wyk’s typology discussion questions classifications that allow an inferred economic purpose to settle what kind of adoption the document records. The methodological issue is whether motive, legal form and practical effect are being conflated. This report does not replace those classifications with a new universal typology. [S09, relevant typology discussion.]

### 4.3 Maintenance, dependency and slavery

The maintenance of Bēltani illustrates an intersection rather than a single status. Her deity-linked title and sisterhood are stated alongside a provision entitlement. The disputed field clause then raises a narrower question about which assets and restrictions support that entitlement. “Usufruct” is a modern analytical interpretation under discussion, not an indigenous word visible in the agreement. [T03; S07; H-C07–08.]

A different document, T20, contains explicit female slave-status language. It would be misleading to dilute that wording into a generic “dependent,” but equally misleading to treat release as necessarily removing every continuing service obligation. The relevant temporal distinctions survive more securely than the restored release expression. This is why a single free/unfree gloss cannot replace analysis of the act, its beneficiaries, its future-claim clauses and its damaged passages. [T20; H-C11.]

Onomastic and devotional expressions require another check: ARAD in a personal name or a seal’s relation to a deity is not an independent human slave-status statement. By contrast, the school witness T25 predicates a human relation using ARAD. These different uses should neither be collapsed nor used to deny the reality of the more explicit status language elsewhere. [T13, T19, T25; H-C12, H-C17.]

### 4.4 Labor, obligations and institutional rights

Payment, service and title answer different questions. Rental a₂ in T09 is not a wage; the interest-free receipts in T04 and T21 do not identify a permanent debtor class; and the damaged agricultural arrangement T15 does not establish the parties’ entire labor status. Equally, a participant’s agrig or nu-eš₃ title does not independently settle ownership or the institutional capacity in which that person contracted. [H-C09–10, H-C15, H-C21.]

The temple-rights examples provide positive evidence beyond cautions. T06 allocates four-month portions; T22 sells an Ekur gatekeeping entitlement specified in days; T23 bundles offices in an annual ten-day turn; T24 combines annual days of Nuska-linked offices with land. These are reasons to read “prebend” as a scholarly description of a documented entitlement, with its own terms, rather than as a synonym for salary, employment or a complete religious profession. Purchase price, potential income, associated land and actual execution of duties remain distinct. [T06, T22–T24; H-C13–14.]

### 4.5 Sumerian writing, Akkadian usage and genre

T12 supplies direct evidence of mixed written expression: Akkadian prepositions and finite verbs occur with Sumerian-written property terms and formulae. T13’s residence clause is likewise syntactically Akkadian despite the logographic É. Neither proves what each named person spoke, and neither licenses expanding the catalogue’s language label into a historical claim about vernacular usage. [H-C02, H-C16.]

The semantic table therefore records **what is written**, not a fabricated bilingual synonym list. An Akkadian scholarly reading of a logogram is different from an attested syllabic Akkadian form. The relevant ePSD2/ORACC and CAD entries could not be systematically consulted; unattested local equivalents are not supplied as if verified. Regional adoption-formula comparison available through Spada is kept in section 6.

The didactic court-case witness PBS 8/2, 173 is valuable precisely as a text using spouse, service/status and assembly expressions. Its school transmission does not establish a newly documented contemporary proceeding, but neither does the label “school text” prove that every narrative element was invented. The question is what this witness can establish. The quarantined CBS 11324 record and the legal obverse missing from the displayed CBS 6098 transcription underscore the need to check actual content before using catalogue associations. [T25; Q01; L01; H-C17.]

## 5. Disagreement, ambiguity, and source limitations

### 5.1 Legal reconstructions that remain contested

**The field in PBS 8/2, 116.** The relevant possessive and the relation between support, alienation and inheritance have produced different reconstructions. As presented by Van Wyk, the issue includes whether the restriction concerns the brothers’ property as security for maintenance or the sister’s field and its disposition during her lifetime. The agreement does not thereby transparently become a Roman-law usufruct or modern life estate. This return preserves the alternatives rather than settling the dispute from a digital transliteration alone. [T03 rev. 1–5; S07, discussion of PBS 8/2, 116; Stone 1982 encountered indirectly.]

**The BE 6/2, 23 dossier.** Van Wyk’s 2019 study surveys reconstructions of how Narubtum, Migir-Enlil, Ur-Pabilsaga and Naramtum were related and how the property arrangement operated. Earlier and later readings differ on such matters as Migir-Enlil’s identification, who adopted whom and the significance of related documents. Van Wyk proposes a particular hybrid use of adoption and division templates. These are competing reconstructions reported through a consulted study, not three independent original studies personally checked here. No family tree or decisive claim for BE 6/2, 23 is entered in the direct-text register. [S08, opening textual discussion and pp. 17–20.]

**“Fictional adoption.”** A property effect does not, as a matter of reasoning, demonstrate that affiliation was socially unreal; nor does kinship language demonstrate affection, co-residence or a particular motive. Classification must be justified from the whole document and relevant context. The current evidence supports that caution more securely than any comprehensive alternative account of adoption purposes. [S09; direct contextual comparisons T01–T02.]

### 5.3 Limits that affect historical conclusions

The full Stone–Owen adoption study, Meinhold’s prebend article and several other key studies were not directly available. Their existence and bibliographic details are not substitutes for their arguments. Conversely, some original digital tablets were available even when a later specialist discussion was not; those observations remain useful within their narrower limits.

The corpus does not support a census of free people, enslaved people, dependents, workers or households. In particular, no comprehensive local account of terms conventionally discussed under awīlum, muškēnum, wardum/amtum, hired labor, tenancy or institutional dependency has been established. These are **coverage gaps**, not assertions that the categories were absent or present in a particular fixed form. The same restraint applies to the precise roles of bur-šu-ma and other specialist office designations.

No full sign collation, linguistic dating study, text-image reconciliation or architectural re-excavation has been performed. Later collations can change a name, clause or date and thus change the social argument. Direct digital consultation is a substantive evidentiary step, but not the last possible verification step.

## 6. Comparative evidence outside Old Babylonian Nippur

**Cotsen 52175 / Education 78: unprovenanced Old Babylonian model contracts, with Larsa or Kutalla attribution proposed by Spada.** The published edition juxtaposes an adoption model and an orchard-sale model. Its oath and formulae underpin a regional attribution; that is not an excavated Nippur provenance. Spada compares the pairing of child-affiliation and heir-making expressions, including šu-ti/leqû and ĝar/šakānu in southern material versus ri/tarû-related Nippur formulae. These comparisons help identify regional conventions, not mechanically interchangeable verbs or distinct universal adoption classes. The models are not counted as realized transactions. [S06, §§2.2–2.3 and notes 45–48.]

**Female religious designations.** The same comparative discussion of nu-gig/qadištu shows why inherited English religious or sexual labels require argument. It does not establish that a Nippur LUKUR had the same role. Differences of term, deity, city and genre must survive comparison. [S06, §2.2 and commentary; direct Nippur contrast T03, T13.]

**Sippar and law-collection analogies.** Van Wyk’s maintenance discussion engages analogies beyond the local corpus, including later legal categories and broader Old Babylonian provisions. Such comparisons may generate questions about support and asset control; they do not prove the legal regime of a Nippur woman. Suurmeijer’s Sippar adoption article and the Culbertson collection remain leads, not sources silently imported as local evidence. [S07; S18–S19.]

No Ur III, Middle Babylonian or Neo-Babylonian text is used as a direct attestation of an Old Babylonian Nippur rule. No historical generalization in this report depends on collapsing those periods.
