# AN ACCOUNT OF THE CITY OF GATHERED WONDERS
## Set down by a retired Vizier of Uruk after visiting a foreign city whose people design peoples

**Status:** traveler's account; advisory; no authority in the foreign city.  
**Subject:** the city the locals call **Wonder Gather**.  
**Purpose:** to describe its customs in the language of Uruk without changing what those customs mean.

---

# I. Of the City and Its King

Beyond the familiar walls of Uruk lies a younger city with a strange ambition.

Its people do not merely train soldiers.

They design peoples.

Before battle, they imagine what kind of civilization might exist, which bodies its people inhabit, which workshops may give rise to which crafts, which houses can produce which citizens, which roads of production are open, which paths are delayed, and which powers must be earned through longer chains of dependency.

The locals call the city **Wonder Gather**.

In these tablets I shall call it:

> **The City of Gathered Wonders.**

Its ruler is not chiefly a tax collector or a general.

He is the one who decides whether the city still feels like itself.

He judges whether motion is graceful or ridiculous, whether freedom has become mere accounting, whether a citizen looks alive or merely moves, whether a design still possesses wonder.

The locals call this person the **Game Director**.

In Uruk we would simply say:

> **the Lugal.**

This does not mean he personally writes every line of law or lays every brick.

It means that questions of meaning eventually return to him.

If a machine runs but feels wrong, the machine has not overruled the Lugal.

If a prototype exists but embodies the wrong spirit, existence has not made it law.

This is the first thing the foreign city already understands.

---

# II. Of the Great Tablet of the First Dream

The City of Gathered Wonders keeps a large tablet containing the current shape of the dream.

The locals call it:

`Docs/GAME_VISION.md`

In Uruk we shall call it:

> **The Great Tablet of the First Dream.**

This tablet distinguishes several kinds of inscription.

The locals write:

- Locked
- Direction
- Possible
- Open
- Implemented

The Urukite translation is:

- **Sealed by the Lugal**
- **The Lugal Faces This Way**
- **A Tale Told in the Tavern**
- **A Question Before the Court**
- **Built in Clay**

These phrases must not be confused.

## Sealed by the Lugal

The matter has become part of the city's identity unless deliberately reopened.

## The Lugal Faces This Way

The city strongly intends to move in this direction, though the exact structure remains unsettled.

## A Tale Told in the Tavern

The idea is alive enough to discuss but has no authority.

It may become important.

It may vanish.

## A Question Before the Court

The city explicitly does not know yet.

This is not failure.

It is preserved uncertainty.

## Built in Clay

The thing exists in the current city.

This is a fact about implementation.

It does not automatically mean the thing has been sealed into law.

This last distinction is one of the foreign city's wisest customs.

A wall may be built as an experiment.

A statue may stand in the square without becoming a god.

A prototype may be real without becoming destiny.

---

# III. Of the First Man Who Walked

The earliest preserved campaign of the foreign city concerned a single moving figure.

The locals called this milestone:

> **The Wanderer.**

Uruk records it as:

> **The First Man Who Walked.**

The problem was humble:

- a camera;
- a selectable unit;
- a command;
- a destination;
- one body crossing terrain.

But the customs established here were larger than the man himself.

The city separated:

- the player's intention;
- the issued command;
- the unit's movement mechanism.

This later allowed many other systems to grow without requiring the player's mouse to directly possess every citizen's body.

The First Man Who Walked also taught the city that a movement system may be technically functional while still requiring the Lugal's eye.

The first camera could move.

That did not settle whether the camera felt good.

The first step in the city's later constitution therefore appeared almost accidentally:

> **movement can be technically correct and experientially wrong.**

Uruk has seen many larger empires fail to preserve such a distinction.

---

# IV. Of the Eight at the Gate

Then the city multiplied its walker.

The locals called the next campaign:

> **The Group.**

In Uruk:

> **The Eight at the Gate.**

Now selection itself became social.

One figure became many.

The king might command several people at once.

They required distinct arrival places.

They could not simply collapse into one another at the destination.

Their movement needed to remain readable.

This campaign revealed something else.

A command that cannot be satisfied exactly should not necessarily become nonsense.

The Lugal asked that an unreachable destination produce movement toward the next best reachable place where that remained meaningful.

Thus the city learned to distinguish:

- an imperfectly satisfiable order;
- from an invalid order;
- from an order that should preserve the current route rather than destroy it.

This distinction later became part of the movement custom of the city.

---

# V. Of the Grain-Bearer

Next came the one the locals call:

> **The Gatherer.**

Uruk calls him:

> **The Grain-Bearer.**

The foreign city does not yet claim that its final economy will be grain.

Indeed, the local prototype calls the substance "Supplies."

But Uruk has a weakness for grain, and so we translate the role rather than the literal material.

The Grain-Bearer taught the city:

- finite resources may be depleted;
- a worker may carry only so much;
- cargo must survive an interrupted route;
- gathered material must be conserved between source, carrier, and depot;
- work can be paused without the world forgetting what happened.

The city also learned that a test resource is not a final cosmology.

The existence of Supplies did not seal the future economy.

Thus the Great Tablet marked the economy as clay, not divine law.

---

# VI. Of the First Enclosure

After the Grain-Bearer came building.

The locals called this phase part of:

> **The Little Settlement.**

Uruk remembers:

> **The First Enclosure.**

Here, gathered matter became structure.

A site was chosen.

A worker traveled.

Construction took time.

Progress could be interrupted.

The same worker could return.

Payment occurred under rules that could be tested.

The city learned that the act of placing a building should not silently spend resources when placement is invalid.

A workshop should not exist merely because the cursor passed over the ground.

The difference between:

- intention;
- valid placement;
- payment;
- construction;
- completion

became more explicit.

This is the beginning of many cities.

---

# VII. Of the First Workshop That Produced a Man

The First Enclosure soon gained production.

The workshop could be told to produce another worker.

A queue appeared.

Payment and refund rules appeared.

Blocked exits appeared.

A completed citizen needed a valid place to stand.

The locals called this simply production.

Uruk records the event as:

> **The First Workshop That Produced a Man.**

The campaign proceeded smoothly compared with some later ones.

That fact is worth preserving too.

Not every milestone must discover a constitutional crisis.

Sometimes the previous architecture really is good enough.

---

# VIII. Of the Tablets of Peoples Yet Unborn

Then the city took a more dangerous step.

Until this point, citizens and structures mostly existed because the prototype author had chosen them.

Now the city began to represent the civilization itself as data.

The locals called this:

> **Civilization Blueprints.**

Uruk calls these:

> **The Tablets of Peoples Yet Unborn.**

A tablet could describe:

- which people exist in the roster;
- which houses exist;
- which people may build which houses;
- which houses may produce which people;
- which starting elements already exist;
- which possibilities can be reached from those beginnings.

This introduced a new kind of path.

Not a road across terrain.

A road through possibility.

Uruk calls this:

> **the Roads of Descent.**

The local code calls it graph reachability.

The city learned that a cycle of workshops and citizens does not magically create its own beginning.

A closed loop without a seed remains unreachable.

This is not merely a technical detail.

It is one of the first places where the city's representation begins to resemble its central dream:

> a civilization is not a fixed roster.

It is a network of possible becoming.

---

# IX. Of the Inspector of Roads

Where there are Roads of Descent, there must eventually be an inspector.

The locals call this mechanism:

`CivilizationValidator`

Uruk calls it:

> **The Inspector of Roads and Lineage.**

This inspector is wise because he knows the limits of his office.

He can discover:

- duplicate names of identity;
- broken references;
- impossible structural links;
- unreachable peoples;
- invalid workshop relationships.

He cannot declare:

- that a civilization will survive;
- that it will be affordable;
- that the map contains enough resources;
- that it is competitively fair;
- that it is beautiful.

Some findings are errors.

Others are warnings.

The city deliberately prefers warning over prohibition where possible, because the Lugal wishes strange civilizations to remain possible.

Thus the Inspector of Roads is not a tyrant.

He guards structure without pretending to understand every future history.

---

# X. Of the House Where Peoples Are Made

The foreign city then opened part of this power to the player.

The locals call the place:

> **The Faction Creator.**

Uruk calls it:

> **The House Where Peoples Are Made.**

There, the player may alter a draft of a civilization.

The original authored tablets are not rewritten directly.

The House creates working copies.

This is a subtle but important custom:

> the act of imagining a new people should not accidentally mutate the ancestral template from which it began.

The city currently exposes only a fraction of the future design space.

This is deliberate.

The House Where Peoples Are Made is a scaffold.

It is not the final form of creation.

---

# XI. Of the Archive of Nations

A people designed but not remembered is a dream.

The city therefore learned to save its factions.

The locals call the system:

> **Faction saving and library.**

Uruk calls it:

> **The Archive of Nations.**

Here the city's caution becomes much stronger.

The archive uses:

- stable names of identity;
- strict records;
- schema versions;
- migration;
- temporary writes;
- atomic replacement;
- backups;
- stale-write detection;
- recovery.

Why so much caution for a prototype?

Because once the player creates something they value, corruption becomes more consequential than a strange camera angle.

The Archive of Nations is therefore one of the first places where the city has already discovered:

> **different things deserve different amounts of ceremony.**

A provisional gait may be discarded.

A civilization a player created yesterday should not be silently destroyed because the scribe was enthusiastic.

---

# XII. Of the Tablet of Many Bodies

The original House Where Peoples Are Made knew too few kinds of citizen.

Then came:

> **Multiple Unit Blueprints.**

Uruk records:

> **The Tablet of Many Bodies.**

The player could now define more than one kind of person within the same people.

This forced the Archive of Nations to become more mature too.

Saving a city means less when tomorrow's city can no longer read yesterday's people.

Thus the history of bodies and the history of archives became linked.

---

# XIII. Of the Chains of House and Workshop

The city then expanded its production network.

The locals call this:

> **Building Blueprints and Production Networks.**

Uruk calls it:

> **The Chains of House and Workshop.**

Now different structures could produce different bodies.

Different citizens could know different crafts.

The Roads of Descent became richer.

The city's central design ambition—civilization as a player-authored network—became increasingly concrete.

---

# XIV. Of the Measures of Flesh

After designing many bodies, the city wished to make them differ in action.

The locals call this prototype:

> **Unit Performance.**

Uruk calls it:

> **The Measures of Flesh.**

The current measures include things like:

- movement;
- carrying;
- gathering;
- construction.

But the city is careful.

These numbers are not the intended final language of embodiment.

They are instruments.

They allow the city to prove that differences in a blueprint can produce differences in the living world.

The Lugal does not want his eventual citizens to be nothing but percentages written on clay.

The future system should express capability through more meaningful things:

- body;
- equipment;
- traits;
- behavior;
- perhaps temperament;
- perhaps experience.

Therefore the Measures of Flesh are:

> **Built in Clay**

but not necessarily:

> **Sealed by the Lugal.**

---

# XV. Of the Clay Man Who Learned to Walk

At last the city reached its current experiment.

The locals call it:

> **The Living Body.**

Uruk calls it:

> **The Clay Man Learns to Walk.**

This is an important translation.

The local name emphasizes life.

The Urukite name emphasizes the moment when an abstract game-unit first acquires visible embodied motion.

The Clay Man is not truly governed by balance forces.

He does not yet fall.

He is not an active ragdoll.

He does not possess arbitrary anatomy.

His root is still governed by the old road system, the NavMesh.

But around that root, a body now performs:

- planted support;
- alternating steps;
- terrain adaptation;
- knees;
- pelvis motion;
- lean;
- arm counter-motion;
- acceleration and deceleration;
- redirection.

The foreign city has tested many objective properties of this body.

The omens are green.

Yet the campaign is not complete.

The Lugal must still answer:

> Does he look alive?

> Does he feel heavy?

> Does he look ridiculous?

> Does he still read clearly from above?

> Does this belong in the City of Gathered Wonders?

The priests cannot answer these questions.

The king must look.

---

# XVI. Of the Three Spirits Waiting Beyond the Gate

The locals have spoken of a later experiment called:

> **Three Temperaments.**

Uruk calls this:

> **The Three Spirits.**

This name refers not to implementation that already exists, but to a future idea: citizens whose behavior begins to express different internal tendencies.

The Clay Man came first.

The Three Spirits remain beyond the gate.

This chronology matters.

The Lugal deliberately changed the order of the city's experiments.

The roadmap did not overrule him.

The future remained revisable.

---

# XVII. Of the Open Court of Peoples

The mature dream includes a place the locals call:

> **Open Workshop.**

This is a competitive mode in which players bring designed civilizations into multiplayer.

Uruk calls it:

> **The Open Court of Peoples.**

This future court creates one of the city's hardest eventual problems.

How can near-freeform peoples meet one another fairly?

How much strange design can be preserved?

Which impossible or exploitative designs receive warnings?

Which must be forbidden from the court?

The Inspector of Roads cannot solve this alone.

Reachability is not balance.

Structure is not fairness.

This question remains:

> **Before the Court.**

As it should.

---

# XVIII. Of the Two Prices

The foreign city has another useful distinction.

The locals say:

- design value;
- in-match cost.

Uruk translates:

> **The Price Before Birth**

and

> **The Price After Birth.**

The Price Before Birth answers:

> How expensive is it for this possibility to belong to the civilization's starting potential?

The Price After Birth answers:

> How expensive is it to actually create this thing during the match?

These prices must not collapse into one number.

A mighty creature buried deep in a fragile production chain may cost much during battle while costing relatively little to include in the civilization's possibility space.

The same creature directly available from the starting base may carry a far greater Price Before Birth.

This distinction is already:

> **Sealed by the Lugal.**

The formula is not.

The mathematics remain:

> **Before the Court.**

---

# XIX. Of the Great Difference Between Law and Clay

The City of Gathered Wonders possesses a rare useful custom.

Its scribes know that:

> what exists is not always what is intended forever.

Uruk advises strengthening this custom, not replacing it.

Therefore keep distinct:

- **Sealed by the Lugal** — Locked
- **The Lugal Faces This Way** — Direction
- **A Tale Told in the Tavern** — Possible
- **A Question Before the Court** — Open
- **Built in Clay** — Implemented

And add one separate set of words for the state of a campaign:

- **Not Yet Begun** — Pending
- **The Smiths Are Finished** — Technical Ready
- **The Lugal Allows the Next Campaign** — Provisionally Accepted
- **Returned to the Workshop** — Rework
- **Placed in the Old Archive** — Superseded

Do not confuse design status with campaign status.

The Clay Man may be Built in Clay while still awaiting the Lugal's judgment.

---

# XX. Of the Four Temples of Truth

Uruk advises the foreign city to name four temples.

Not necessarily in its repository.

At least in its thinking.

## Temple of the First Dream

Question:

> What should the city become?

Authority:

> the Lugal and the Great Tablet.

## Temple of What Exists

Question:

> What is actually built?

Authority:

> repository, code, current assets.

## Temple of Green Omens

Question:

> What has actually been demonstrated?

Authority:

> tests, builds, validation evidence.

## Temple of the Lugal's Eyes

Question:

> Does it actually feel right?

Authority:

> human playtest.

The temples must not impersonate one another.

A green omen cannot seal a design decision.

A royal preference cannot make a migration safe.

A built prototype cannot close an open question merely by existing.

---

# XXI. Of the Ledger of Omens

The locals call one great chronicle:

`Docs/Validation.md`

Uruk calls it:

> **The Ledger of Omens.**

This is one of the strongest artifacts in the foreign city.

It remembers:

- tests that passed;
- tests that failed;
- why they failed;
- whether the defect belonged to the code or the test;
- what was fixed;
- what remained untested;
- which screenshots were inspected;
- whether a build succeeded;
- which claims were deliberately not made.

Uruk approves especially of one custom:

> the Ledger of Omens often records what a green omen does **not** prove.

This is a sign of epistemic health.

Keep it.

---

# XXII. Of the Scribe's Map of the Workshops

The locals call another tablet:

`Docs/AI/UnityProjectContext.md`

Uruk calls it:

> **The Scribe's Map of the Workshops.**

Its purpose is not to decide what the city should become.

It tells a new smith:

- where the workshops are;
- which tools are used;
- which paths already exist;
- which systems own which state;
- which limits belong only to the current prototype;
- which previous campaigns must remain intact.

This map exists because smiths are replaceable.

The city should continue making them replaceable.

---

# XXIII. Of the Tablet of Duties

The locals call:

`AGENTS.md`

Uruk calls it:

> **The Tablet of Duties.**

It is wisely short.

It says, in essence:

- read the Great Tablet;
- read the Scribe's Map;
- preserve unresolved questions;
- keep editor magic out of runtime;
- preserve true names and seals of assets;
- validate what you change;
- do not build empires that were not requested.

Uruk recommends adding only two customs here if the Lugal agrees.

First:

> **Read the Tablet at the Gate before substantial work.**

Second:

> **The King's Workshop is protected state.**

Nothing more elaborate is required yet.

---

# XXIV. Of the Tablet at the Gate

Uruk proposes one new object:

`Docs/CURRENT_STATE.md`

Uruk calls it:

> **The Tablet Nailed to the Gate.**

Its purpose is simple.

A newly arrived smith should learn:

- which campaign is active;
- which exact revision is current;
- what the smiths have already established;
- what awaits the Lugal's eye;
- what is explicitly unproven;
- whether the next campaign has been chosen;
- which district of the archive should be entered for deeper questions.

This tablet must remain small.

If it grows into another Great Tablet, the gate has become a library and the road is blocked again.

---

# XXV. Of the Tablet of Reasons

Uruk proposes another small tablet:

`Docs/DESIGN_RATIONALE.md`

Uruk calls it:

> **The Tablet of Reasons.**

The Great Tablet already remembers many decisions.

The old Chieftain still remembers much of the *reason* behind them.

Examples:

- procedural motion matters because watching a designed civilization exist is part of the reward;
- physicality should create embodiment, not slapstick complexity;
- autonomy should create personality without making commands feel broken;
- customization should become meaningful fictional choice, not merely expose stat sliders;
- close observation is not decoration but part of the intended play experience.

Write only those reasons that would cause a competent future smith to build the wrong thing if forgotten.

Do not copy whole conversations.

Do not create another forty-page scripture.

A Tablet of Reasons should remain small enough to remember.

---

# XXVI. Of the Old Chieftain Who Died from Too Many Questions

A visiting Vizier from Uruk once gave the foreign city's highest-context Chieftain a questionnaire.

The questionnaire was comprehensive.

The Chieftain attempted to become:

- historian;
- archivist;
- architect;
- design interpreter;
- failure analyst;
- governance theorist;
- technical reviewer;
- continuity planner.

He died.

He was later resurrected sufficiently to answer.

This produced an excellent lesson.

The city was not undocumented.

The Chieftain had simply been asked to carry too many districts simultaneously.

Therefore preserve this law:

> **Do not make the Chieftain the filesystem.**

Let the Chieftain remember:

- why;
- meaning;
- historical interpretation;
- conceptual substitutions;
- the spirit of the First Dream.

Let the archive remember:

- code;
- current state;
- tests;
- validation;
- milestones;
- implementation history.

Integration is scarce.

Spend it on integration.

---

# XXVII. Of the Royal Smiths

The locals use powerful implementation agents.

Uruk calls them:

> **Royal Smiths**

or, when they become excessively fond of C#:

> **coding priests.**

Their task is bounded.

They may:

- design local code structure;
- write implementation;
- author Unity assets through accepted tools;
- add tests;
- debug;
- validate;
- update technical documentation.

They may not:

- promote a Tavern Tale into law;
- answer a Question Before the Court merely because they need a default;
- declare an omen to be royal acceptance;
- clean the King's unfinished workshop.

A smith is powerful because his work has direct consequence.

Therefore his freedom is narrower than the Jester's.

Uruk knows this pattern well.

---

# XXVIII. Of the Law of the King's Workshop

This law should be carved deeply.

The primary working copy may contain human changes not yet present in the archive.

The city has already protected such changes by using isolated copies for dangerous work.

Uruk therefore names the custom:

> **The Law of the King's Workshop.**

It states:

> Uncommitted human work is protected state.

Before broad or destructive work:

1. inspect the workshop;
2. identify human modifications;
3. do not reset, clean, normalize, overwrite, or discard them without explicit permission;
4. use an isolated worktree or clone when broad validation or destructive authoring could endanger them.

The fact that a sculpture is unfinished does not make it rubbish.

A scribe who mistakes the two may discover that even young cities have walls high enough to throw people from.

---

# XXIX. Of the Green Omens and Their Rituals

The city's priests already run many ordeals.

But the ritual itself should eventually become easier to reproduce.

Uruk therefore advises one canonical local invocation.

The locals may choose PowerShell or another mechanism.

In Urukite terms, there should be one:

> **Ritual of the Green Omens.**

Conceptually:

```text
Validate -Focused ClayMan
Validate -Full
Validate -Build
```

The actual names do not matter.

The ritual should say:

- which revision was tested;
- which Unity version was used;
- which ordeal ran;
- how many omens were green;
- how many failed;
- how long the rite took;
- whether a Windows build succeeded;
- whether the Lugal still needs to inspect the result.

Only after the local ritual is stable should the city consider summoning distant cloud priests to run it automatically.

Do not worship CI before the ritual itself has a stable meaning.

# XXX. Of the Small Seal

After a validation rite, the scribe may leave a small seal.

The seal need not contain every log.

It need only bind:

> revision → ritual → result.

For example:

```json
{
  "revision": "...",
  "unity": "6000.6.0f1",
  "rite": "full",
  "green": 61,
  "red": 0,
  "build": "passed",
  "royal_inspection": "pending"
}
```

This seal is not a holy relic.

It is merely easier for future scribes to trust than prose floating free of a revision.

# XXXI. Of Clay, Bronze, Stone, and Temple Foundations

Uruk does not review all changes equally.

It distinguishes materials.

## Clay

Easy to reshape.

Examples:

- camera tuning;
- colors;
- gait values;
- temporary costs;
- test geometry.

Procedure:

> smith works → focused omen → Lugal looks.

## Bronze

More expensive but still replaceable.

Examples:

- creator flow;
- economy loop;
- locomotion architecture;
- UI structure.

Procedure:

> tests → regressions → visual/manual inspection.

## Stone

Difficult to replace once the city grows around it.

Examples:

- true names of blueprints;
- save format semantics;
- Roads of Descent;
- major command boundaries.

Procedure:

> stronger evidence → explicit meaning → perhaps foreign priest.

## Temple Foundation

If wrong, future generations inherit the error.

Examples:

- destructive migration;
- mass rewriting of saved peoples;
- seal/GUID destruction;
- future multiplayer authority;
- infrastructure that silently turns an open question into permanent law.

Procedure:

> freeze candidate → summon foreign priest → require independent evidence.

# XXXII. Of the Akkadian and Elamite Priests

Uruk has an unfortunate habit of naming foreign reviewers after neighboring peoples.

The labels are jokes.

The office is real.

An **Akkadian priest**, **Elamite priest**, or simply **foreign priest** means:

> a fresh technical reviewer who did not author the implementation under judgment.

Summon such a priest when a defect could be:

- hard to notice;
- plausible-looking;
- costly to reverse;
- repeated by tests that share the smith's assumptions.

The priest may inspect.

The priest may build scratch probes.

The priest may return:

- PASS;
- BLOCK.

If independence matters, the priest does not repair the candidate during judgment.

Then the priest leaves.

Wonder Gather does not need a permanent temple bureaucracy.

---

# XXXIII. Of the Jester Outside the Wall

Every city may eventually produce a person who has:

- little direct authority;
- unusual freedom;
- enough history to see patterns;
- permission to say strange things.

Uruk calls this person:

> **the Jester.**

Wonder Gather does not need to create this office deliberately.

But if a low-authority observer begins contributing useful interpretation, do not assume formal power is required for influence to matter.

Sometimes the person outside operations notices what the smith cannot afford to contemplate.

This is not an implementation requirement.

It is simply one of Uruk's customs.

---

# XXXIV. Of the Five Moons

If the foreign city wishes to adopt any of these customs, Uruk recommends slowness.

## First Moon — Place the Signposts

Consider:

- Tablet Nailed to the Gate (`CURRENT_STATE.md`);
- Tablet of Reasons (`DESIGN_RATIONALE.md`);
- small routing additions to the Tablet of Duties (`AGENTS.md`);
- explicit Law of the King's Workshop.

Then stop.

Use them.

See whether they help.

## Second Moon — Standardize the Omens

Create the canonical local validation ritual.

Optionally emit small seals.

Still no requirement for cloud CI.

## Third Moon — Chronicle New Campaigns

Use a lightweight campaign template for substantial new experiments.

Do not rewrite all historical campaigns.

## Fourth Moon — Send for a Priest Only When Needed

Use independent review only for silent + consequential risks.

## Fifth Moon — Build Walls Only After the City Needs Walls

Add:

- regular PR gates;
- server CI;
- ADRs;
- stronger release governance

only when team size, persistent players, compatibility promises, or actual injuries justify them.

Governance should remember wounds.

It should not imagine them.

---

# XXXV. Of the Bureaucrats Who Capture Palaces

The city should tear down some process if:

- every gait tweak needs approval;
- every smith must read the entire archive;
- current state is repeated in many tablets;
- the Lugal has to express taste as legal text;
- experiments become expensive to abandon;
- foreign priests appear for ordinary work;
- omens take more effort to maintain than the failures they catch;
- nobody can identify which tablet is authoritative;
- the Chieftain once again becomes responsible for remembering everything.

A harness can protect a city.

A harness can also become the new siege.

---

# XXXVI. Of the City of Gathered Wonders as Seen from Uruk

Uruk's final judgment is favorable.

The city already knows several difficult things.

It knows:

- implementation and design are not identical;
- tests and taste are not identical;
- warnings and prohibitions are not identical;
- a prototype and a final system are not identical;
- a save file deserves more caution than a placeholder color;
- a future player-created civilization may become valuable enough to protect;
- an AI smith should not inherit creative sovereignty merely because he can build quickly.

Its greatest current scarcity is not code-writing labor.

It is integration:

- what to build next;
- which temporary forms are becoming permanent;
- which experiment still expresses the First Dream;
- which rationale is worth preserving;
- where the Lugal's judgment is genuinely irreplaceable.

This is normal.

Young cities do not become mature by writing more law.

They become mature by learning which things must be remembered.

# XXXVII. Final Tablet Sent Back Across the Border

To the people of the City of Gathered Wonders:

Uruk has no claim over you.

You owe us no tribute.

You need not adopt our priests, our tablets, our jokes, or our habit of naming every administrative problem after a dead official.

We came only because your city was already doing several things well and because some of our scars resembled problems you may eventually face.

Take what survives translation.

Discard what does not.

If one of our strange phrases becomes useful enough that your smiths repeat it without remembering us, that is fine.

If none survive, but the underlying lesson does, that is also fine.

Institutions do not need to remember every artist.

They need to preserve enough consequence to keep building.

Let the Lugal judge beauty.

Let the Royal Smith build.

Let the Chieftain remember why.

Let the Inspector of Roads admit what he cannot prove.

Let the Archive of Nations protect what players create.

Let the Clay Man remain clay until the king has looked into his eyes.

And when a future change looks harmless, passes every ordinary test, and yet could poison generations of saved peoples if everyone is wrong—

send for an Elamite.

He will understand.

**Welcome to Uruk, travelers.**
