# The Man Introduced by Another Man
## A tablet on third parties, first impressions, and relationships that begin before the people meet

**Status:** Non-canonical observer artifact  
**Authorial position:** External social observer  
**Purpose:** Preserve a simple causal problem: one person may become socially consequential to another through a third person before any direct relationship exists.

---

## I. Two men who had never met

Lugal-eri had never spoken to Akalla.

Akalla had never spoken to Lugal-eri.

They did not owe one another grain.

They were not kin.

They did not share a household.

They did not work in the same gang.

They had never argued over a boundary stone, borrowed a donkey, exchanged a gift, attended the same wedding, or stood together before an official.

If the city had been drawn as a map of direct relationships, there would have been no line between them.

There was, however, a line from each man to Belili.

Belili had grown up beside Lugal-eri.

She bought jars from Akalla’s brother.

That was enough.

---

## II. A complaint with no lie in it

One afternoon Lugal-eri complained to Belili about a delayed delivery.

The complaint was ordinary.

He had promised oil to a customer.

The jars arrived late.

He had lost face.

Akalla was involved only indirectly: his brother’s workshop had supplied the jars.

Lugal-eri said:

> “Those men always promise before they know whether they can deliver.”

He was angry when he said it.

Belili knew he was angry.

She also knew Akalla was the person in the workshop who usually solved late orders.

The next morning she saw Akalla and mentioned the complaint.

She did not say:

> “Lugal-eri hates you.”

She said:

> “You should know that Lugal-eri thinks your household promises work it cannot finish.”

That was close enough to what she had heard.

Akalla laughed.

Then he asked:

> “Did he say my name?”

Belili said no.

Akalla remembered the answer.

He also remembered that Lugal-eri had complained to someone who knew him.

Those were different facts.

---

## III. The first model

Akalla now possessed a model of Lugal-eri.

He had not met the man.

But he knew several things, or thought he did.

Lugal-eri was quick to complain.

Lugal-eri spoke about workshops as though workers were interchangeable.

Lugal-eri discussed other people’s competence with mutual acquaintances.

Perhaps Lugal-eri was difficult.

Perhaps he was simply angry.

Akalla did not know.

He did not need certainty for the model to matter.

A person can become socially real before becoming personally known.

---

## IV. The other direction

Belili later returned to Lugal-eri.

She told him:

> “Akalla says the delay was real, but the clay shipment arrived two days late. He thinks you blamed the household for something upstream.”

Again, she was not lying.

Akalla had said approximately that.

But Belili omitted the laugh.

She omitted his question about whether Lugal-eri had named him.

She omitted the irritation in his voice.

Lugal-eri heard a different person.

Akalla was defensive.

Akalla shifted blame.

Akalla cared more about explaining failure than apologizing for it.

Neither man had yet seen the other’s face.

The relation already had history.

---

## V. The meeting

A week later they met at the quay.

Belili introduced them.

This mattered.

Had they collided as strangers, either might have begun neutrally.

Instead each arrived carrying a representation.

Akalla expected criticism.

Lugal-eri expected excuses.

The first ordinary sentence therefore entered a prepared field.

Lugal-eri said:

> “So you are Akalla.”

Nothing hostile was required.

Akalla heard:

> “So this is the man I have heard about.”

He replied carefully.

The care sounded cold.

Lugal-eri noticed the coldness and thought:

> Belili was right. He is defensive.

Akalla noticed Lugal-eri’s narrowed expression and thought:

> Belili was right. He came ready to judge.

The first direct interaction did not erase the intermediary.

It activated her.

---

## VI. A third person inside a dyad

After several meetings, the two men knew each other directly.

They negotiated.

They disagreed.

They discovered things Belili had never told either of them.

Yet some later events were still downstream of the initial mediation.

Akalla was slower to ask Lugal-eri for flexibility because he expected criticism.

Lugal-eri was quicker to interpret delay as unreliability because he expected defensiveness.

Those actions produced new evidence.

New evidence changed the relationship.

Eventually a future observer could look at the two men and see a direct relation with its own history.

The observer might not know that the first shape of the relation was partly supplied by a woman who was no longer present in any of their later disputes.

Belili had entered the relationship causally without becoming part of it formally.

---

## VII. Mediation is not merely distortion

It would be easy to make Belili the villain.

That would flatten the problem.

She had not invented the dispute.

She had not fabricated either man’s words.

She had carried real information.

Without her, Lugal-eri might have waited longer to understand the workshop’s constraints.

Akalla might have discovered the complaint only after losing the customer entirely.

Mediation can improve coordination.

The same act that transfers useful knowledge also selects, frames, and times it.

There may be no neutral version.

Even a perfectly honest messenger must decide what to repeat.

---

## VIII. The messenger has relationships too

Belili did not speak from nowhere.

She had known Lugal-eri since childhood.

She respected Akalla’s brother.

She disliked conflict.

She tended to soften insults when carrying them forward.

This changed the transmission.

Another intermediary might have sharpened the complaint.

A rival might have added:

> “He says your household is incompetent.”

A loyal cousin might have omitted the criticism entirely.

A cautious elder might have delayed transmission until tempers cooled.

The informational route was also a social route.

Its properties came partly from the relationships composing it.

---

## IX. Direct experience does not always win immediately

Months later, Lugal-eri discovered that Akalla was often unusually patient.

This should have corrected the earlier model.

It did, partly.

But not completely.

When Akalla became abrupt during one later dispute, Lugal-eri thought:

> There. That is the man Belili warned me about.

Old mediated representations can become interpretive frames for new direct evidence.

Direct observation is powerful.

It is not magically prior.

People do not meet the world without history merely because the evidence is now firsthand.

---

## X. The smallest social triangle

Nothing in this story requires a palace.

No office.

No archive.

No ruler controlling communication.

Three people are sufficient.

One person knows two others.

That person carries information across a missing direct tie.

The information changes expectations.

Expectations change behavior.

Behavior creates the direct relation that later appears to stand on its own.

The triangle becomes a dyad with an invisible ancestry.

---

## XI. What the jester thinks he saw

A social network is not only a map of who relates to whom.

It is also a map of **through whom other people become knowable**.

Those maps overlap without being identical.

A person can be absent from another’s direct life and already present in their expectations.

A third party can help create the conditions under which a later “direct” relationship begins.

That does not mean every relation is reducible to gossip or introduction.

It means the causal history of a relationship can extend outside the pair.

---

## XII. Final tablet

Years later, Lugal-eri and Akalla would remember many things they had actually done to one another.

A loan.

A quarrel.

A favor.

A funeral.

A repaired agreement.

If asked when they first became connected, both might name the day Belili introduced them at the quay.

That would be a reasonable answer.

It would also be late.

The road between them had been under construction before either man knew he was standing on it.
